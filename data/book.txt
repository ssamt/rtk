Romance of the Three Kingdoms


Chapter 1
Three heroes swear brotherhood in the peach garden
Against Yellow Turbans heroes win their first merit

A lyric runs:
    The Yangtze eastward rolls its endless flow,
    Whose waves have washed the heroes long ago.
    A turn of head, and all their labor vain:
    The right, the wrong, the conqueror, the slain.
    Yet ageless still the verdant mountains stand,
    How oft the sunset's blaze has stained the land!
    The hoary fishers, woodmen on the shore,
    Watch springtime breezes, autumn moons pass o'er.
    With cloudy wine, glad friends together meet;
    All deeds of ages melt in laughter sweet.

Such is the way of the realm: long divided, must unite; long united, must divide. At the close of Zhou the Seven States warred, until Qin swallowed them all. When Qin fell, Chu and Han contended in their turn, and Chu gave way. Han rose when Gaozu cut down the white serpent and took up arms, and held the realm entire. Guangwu restored it in a later age, and the line ran on to Emperor Xian, in whose day it broke into three kingdoms. The disorder may be traced to the two emperors Huan and Ling. Huan shut away men of virtue and gave his trust to eunuchs. When he died and Ling came to the throne, the Grand General Dou Wu and the Grand Tutor Chen Fan governed as regents. In those days the eunuch Cao Jie and his fellows had the run of the palace. Dou Wu and Chen Fan laid a plan to destroy them, but their counsel leaked, and they were destroyed themselves. From that day the eunuchs knew no check.

On the full moon of the fourth month, the second year of Jianning, the Emperor held court in the Wende Hall. He had scarcely taken his seat when a gale sprang up in the corner of the hall, and a great green serpent dropped from the roof beams and coiled upon the throne. The Emperor started back and fell; his attendants bore him within, and the officials scattered. In a moment the serpent was gone. Then thunder and rain broke, with hail behind them, and did not cease till midnight, wrecking houses beyond counting. In the second month of the fourth year, Luoyang quaked, and the sea rose over the coast and swept its people away. In the first year of Guanghe a hen turned cock. On the first of the sixth month a black vapor more than ten zhang long flew into the Wende Hall, and in the seventh month a rainbow was seen in the Jade Hall, while the cliffs at Wuyuan broke apart. Such portents came thick, and of more kinds than one.

By edict the Emperor asked his ministers the cause of these calamities. The Court Counselor Cai Yong sent up a forthright memorial: the rainbow and the hen that turned cock came of women and eunuchs meddling in government. The Emperor read it, sighed, and rose to change his robes. Cao Jie, standing behind him, stole a look at the paper and carried every word to his fellows. They contrived a charge against Cai Yong and had him sent back to his village. In time ten of them leagued together in their villainy — Zhang Rang, Zhao Zhong, Feng Xu, Duan Gui, Cao Jie, Hou Lan, Jian Shuo, Cheng Kuang, Xia Yun, Guo Sheng — and were called the Ten Attendants. Zhang Rang the Emperor honored and trusted, and addressed as Father. Government rotted by the day, till the realm's heart turned to revolt and brigands rose in swarms.


In the commandery of Julu lived three brothers: Zhang Jiao, Zhang Bao, and Zhang Liang. Zhang Jiao had been a scholar who failed the examinations for office. He was in the hills one day gathering herbs when he came upon an old man, green of eye and young of face, leaning on a staff of goosefoot wood. The old man called him into a cave and put into his hands three Heavenly scrolls. "These are the Essential Arts of Taiping," he said. "With them you are to preach in Heaven's stead and deliver the world. Let another purpose stir in you, and the retribution will be certain." Zhang Jiao bowed and asked his name. "I am the Old Immortal of Nanhua," he answered, and with the word he turned to a breath of clear wind and was gone.


Zhang Jiao studied the book day and night until he could call up the wind and summon rain, and men named him the Taiping Daoist. In the first month of the first year of Zhongping, plague walked the land. Zhang Jiao went about with charmed water, healing the sick, and gave himself the name of Great Worthy Master. Five hundred disciples and more followed him, drifting like cloud through the four quarters, each of them able to use charms and chants. More came in daily, till he set them in thirty-six divisions — the greater above ten thousand men, the lesser six or seven thousand — and put a captain over each, with the title of General. They spread words: "The Azure Heaven is dead; the Yellow Heaven shall rise." And again: "The year stands at jiazi; the realm shall have great fortune." The people were bidden to write those two characters in white clay upon their gates. Through the eight provinces of Qing, You, Xu, Ji, Jing, Yang, Yan, and Yu, every household revered the name of the Great Worthy Master Zhang Jiao. He sent one of his party, Ma Yuanyi, secretly with gold and silk, to win the eunuch Feng Xu for their man inside. To his brothers he said: "The hardest thing to come by is the heart of the people. It would be a pity not to ride this tide and take the realm." So yellow banners were sewn in secret and a day set for the rising, while the disciple Tang Zhou was sent with a letter for Feng Xu. Tang Zhou rode instead to the palace and laid the whole plot bare. The Emperor summoned the Grand General He Jin, who seized Ma Yuanyi and struck off his head. Feng Xu and the rest were gathered in and thrown into prison.


Word came to Zhang Jiao that he was discovered, and he was under arms the same night. He styled himself General of Heaven, and named Zhang Bao General of Earth and Zhang Liang General of Men. To the crowds he declared: "The fortune of Han is running out, and a great sage is come. Bow to the will of Heaven and rejoice in Taiping." From every quarter the common people bound yellow turbans about their heads and came over to him, some four or five hundred thousand of them. The rebel power swelled beyond measure, and the imperial troops broke at the rumor of it. He Jin asked an edict of the Emperor with all speed, bidding every district look to its defenses and win merit against the rebels. Three Generals of the Household — Lu Zhi, Huangfu Song, and Zhu Jun — went out with picked troops, each by his own road.


Now one of Zhang Jiao's armies came down upon the borders of You Province. Its Grand Administrator was Liu Yan, a man of Jingling in Jiangxia and a descendant of Prince Gong of Lu. He heard what was coming upon him and called in the Colonel Zou Jing to take counsel. Zou Jing said: "They are many and we are few. You should raise men at once to meet them, Your Excellency." Liu Yan agreed, and put out a call for volunteers. The proclamation came to Zhuo County, and there it drew out a hero.


The man was no great reader; he was easy and mild by nature, sparing of speech, and neither joy nor anger came into his face, but his mind had always run on great designs, and he loved above all to make friends of the bold spirits of the realm. He stood seven chi and five cun. His ears came down to his shoulders and his hands below his knees, and his eyes could look upon his own ears; his face was like jade in a cap, his lips as though rouged. He came of Liu Sheng, Prince Jing of Zhongshan, and through him of Emperor Jing of Han. His name was Liu Bei, his style Xuande. Liu Sheng's son Liu Zhen had been made Marquis of Zhuolu in the days of Emperor Wu, and lost the title afterwards when his gold for the imperial sacrifice was found wanting, and so this branch of the house was left behind in Zhuo County. Liu Bei's grandfather was Liu Xiong, his father Liu Hong, who was put forward as a Xiaolian and held some small office, but died young. Liu Bei was left fatherless as a boy, and served his mother dutifully. The house was poor, and they lived by selling straw sandals and weaving mats. Their village was Lousang. Southeast of the house stood a mulberry more than five zhang high, that from afar showed like the canopy of a carriage. A reader of faces had once said: "Out of this house will come a man of rank."


As a child Liu Bei played with the village boys under that tree, and told them: "When I become the Son of Heaven, I shall ride under a canopy like this." His uncle Liu Yuanqi wondered at the words. "This is no common boy," he said. He saw Liu Bei's family in poverty and helped them often. When Liu Bei was fifteen his mother sent him away to study. Zheng Xuan and Lu Zhi were his teachers, and among his friends was Gongsun Zan. He was twenty-eight when Liu Yan's proclamation went up. He read it that day and heaved a long sigh. A voice behind him said sharply: "What is all this sighing, when a true man should be lending the country his strength?"


Liu Bei turned. The man stood eight chi, with a leopard's head and great round eyes, a swallow's jaw and a tiger's whiskers, a voice like thunder and the force of a galloping horse. Liu Bei asked the name of so strange a figure. "Zhang is my surname, Fei my name, Yide my style," said he. "My people have been in Zhuo Commandery many generations and hold a fair estate there. I sell wine and butcher pigs, and I like to make friends of bold men everywhere. I asked because I saw you sigh over the proclamation just now." "I am of the imperial house of Han, Liu Bei by name," said the other. "The Yellow Turbans are up, and I would gladly break them and give the people peace, but I sighed because I have not the means." "I have money by me," said Zhang Fei. "Let us raise a company out of the villages and go into this great business together. What do you say?" It pleased Liu Bei well, and the two went off to a village tavern to drink.


They were still at their wine when a big man pushed a barrow up to the door, set it down, and came in and sat. "Wine here, and be quick," he called. "I am for the city to take service." Liu Bei looked at him. He was nine chi tall, with a beard of two chi, a face like a ripe date and lips as though rouged, the eyes of a red phoenix and brows like silkworms at rest, and a presence stately and commanding. Liu Bei made room for him at the table and asked his name. "Guan is my surname, Yu my name. My style was Shouchang, later changed to Yunchang. I am from Jieliang in Hedong. There was a bully at home who traded on his power to grind the people, and I killed him. I have been five or six years on the road since. I heard there was a levy here against the rebels, and I have come to enlist." Liu Bei told him his purpose, and Guan Yu was glad of it. Together they went out to Zhang Fei's farm to lay their plans.


Zhang Fei said: "Behind my farm there is a peach garden, and the blossom is at its height. Let us sacrifice there tomorrow to Heaven and Earth, and the three of us be made brothers, of one heart and one strength. Then we may look to great things." Liu Bei and Guan Yu agreed. Next day they made ready in the garden a black bull, a white horse, and all else the rite asked for, and the three burned incense, bowed twice, and swore together: "We, Liu Bei, Guan Yu and Zhang Fei, though of different surnames, are this day made brothers. Of one heart and strength, we will succor the distressed and hold up the falling. Above we will serve the state, and below give the people peace. We ask not birth in the same year and month, but pray to die together on one same day. August Heaven and Sovereign Earth, look upon these hearts. If any turn from his faith or forget his debt, let Heaven and man cut him down." The oath made, they bowed to Liu Bei as eldest, Guan Yu next, Zhang Fei youngest. The sacrifice done, they killed an ox and set out wine, gathered the brave men of the countryside, above three hundred of them, and drank deep in the peach garden. Next day they got their arms in order, but they had no horses to ride.


They were still turning the matter over when word came that two strangers with a train of men were driving a herd of horses up to the farm. "Heaven is with us!" said Liu Bei, and the three went out to meet them. They proved to be great merchants of Zhongshan, Zhang Shiping and Su Shuang, who went north every year to trade in horses and had turned back this season because of the rising. Liu Bei brought them in, set wine before them, and told them of his wish to put down the rebels and give the people peace. The merchants were delighted, and made him a present of fifty good horses, with five hundred liang of gold and silver and a thousand jin of fine steel for his armory. Liu Bei thanked them and saw them off, and set good smiths to work. For himself they forged a pair of swords, and for Guan Yu the Green Dragon Crescent Blade, also called the Frost Fair Blade, which weighed eighty-two jin. Zhang Fei had a steel-pointed spear of one zhang eight. Each had his suit of mail. Five hundred men and more of the countryside came in, and with these they went to Zou Jing, who brought them before the Grand Administrator Liu Yan. When they had made their bows and given their names, Liu Bei spoke of his descent, and Liu Yan was overjoyed and recognized him as his nephew.


Within a few days came word that the Yellow Turban captain Cheng Yuanzhi was upon Zhuo Commandery with fifty thousand men. Liu Yan sent Zou Jing out with the three brothers and their five hundred soldiers to break him. They marched gladly, and came up with the rebels under Mount Daxing, a host with their hair loose and yellow turbans bound across their brows. The two lines faced one another. Liu Bei rode out between Guan Yu on his left and Zhang Fei on his right, and raising his whip he cried: "Rebels against the throne, lay down your arms while you may!"


Cheng Yuanzhi in his rage sent out his lieutenant Deng Mao. Zhang Fei leveled the Serpent Spear and rode straight at him. His arm rose, the point took Deng Mao in the breast, and the man went over backwards out of the saddle. Cheng Yuanzhi saw him fall, whipped his horse forward and came at Zhang Fei with his blade whirling. But Guan Yu swung the great blade and flew to meet him, and Cheng Yuanzhi took fright at the sight of him. He had no time to put up his guard, and one stroke cut him in two. Men of later days praised the pair in a poem:
    This day beholds the heroes' worth displayed:
    One tries the spear, the other tries the blade.
    In the first fight, dread power they unfold;
    When realms are three, their names shall be enrolled.


The rebels saw their captain cut down, threw away their arms and ran. Liu Bei waved his men on after them. More surrendered than could be counted, and he came home with a great victory. Liu Yan rode out himself to meet them and rewarded the soldiers. Next day a dispatch came from Gong Jing, Grand Administrator of Qing Province: the Yellow Turbans had ringed his city about and it was near falling, and he begged for relief. Liu Yan took counsel with Liu Bei, who said: "Let me go." So Liu Yan put five thousand men under Zou Jing, who marched to Qing Province with Liu Bei, Guan Yu and Zhang Fei. The rebels saw the relief come up, divided their force and fought them in a scramble. Liu Bei had too few men to prevail, and drew back thirty li and camped. "They are many and we are few," he said to Guan Yu and Zhang Fei. "Nothing but a surprise will win this." So Guan Yu took a thousand men into ambush on the left of the hill and Zhang Fei a thousand on the right, and the gong was to be the signal for both to come out together.


Next day Liu Bei and Zou Jing went forward with drums beating and men shouting. The rebels came out to meet them, and Liu Bei drew off. They pressed after him, and no sooner were they over the ridge than the gong sounded through his ranks. The two companies broke from left and right, Liu Bei wheeled about and came at them again, and caught between the three the rebel host went to pieces. They were driven right up to the walls of the provincial city, where Gong Jing led out his militia to join the fight. The rebels were badly beaten and cut down in great numbers, and the siege of Qing Province was raised. A later poet wrote of Liu Bei:
    He planned and reckoned with a power divine;
    Where dragons fly, two tigers must resign.
    His maiden field a mighty triumph showed;
    Though poor, he'd share the realm by fate bestowed.


When Gong Jing had feasted the troops, Zou Jing made ready to go home. "I hear that the General of the Household Lu Zhi is engaged with the rebel chief Zhang Jiao at Guangzong," said Liu Bei. "I was his pupil once, and I would go and help him." So Zou Jing took his army back, and Liu Bei with Guan Yu and Zhang Fei led his own five hundred to Guangzong. He came into Lu Zhi's camp, made his bow in the tent and told his errand. Lu Zhi was well pleased, and kept him at headquarters to await orders.


Zhang Jiao had a hundred and fifty thousand men, Lu Zhi fifty thousand, and the two lay facing each other at Guangzong, neither yet prevailing. Lu Zhi said to Liu Bei: "I have the rebel chief hemmed in here. His brothers Zhang Liang and Zhang Bao are at Yingchuan, drawn up against Huangfu Song and Zhu Jun. Take your own men — I will add a thousand of the imperial troops — and go to Yingchuan for news, and we will fix a day to close upon them." Liu Bei took his orders and rode for Yingchuan through the night. Huangfu Song and Zhu Jun were holding the rebels there, and the rebels, getting the worst of it, had fallen back on Changshe and pitched their camp among the long grass. Huangfu Song said to Zhu Jun: "They have camped in the grass. Fire will serve." So every man was set to bind a bundle of straw, and they lay hidden. That night a great wind rose. After the second watch the fires were set all together, and the two generals each led his troops against the camp. As the flame went up to heaven, the rebels broke in terror, horses unsaddled and men unarmored, and ran every way. The killing lasted till daylight, when Zhang Liang and Zhang Bao gathered the remnant and cut a road out.


Of a sudden a troop of horse under red banners came up and barred the way. At the head of it rode a general seven chi tall, with small eyes and a long beard, a Cavalry Commandant and a man of Qiao Commandery in the Principality of Pei: Cao Cao by name, Mengde by style. His father Cao Song had been born a Xiahou, and took the name of Cao as the adopted son of the Attendant Cao Teng. Cao Cao's childhood name was Aman, and he was also called Jili. As a child he loved hunting, singing and dancing, and he was cunning and resourceful. He had an uncle who could not bear his wild ways and complained of him to Cao Song, who reproved him. Then the boy hit on a trick. When his uncle next came by, he dropped to the ground and feigned a fit. The uncle ran in alarm to Cao Song, who came in haste and found the boy as well as ever. "Your uncle says you had a fit," said Cao Song. "Are you well again already?" "I have never had any such illness," said Cao Cao. "I have lost my uncle's love, and so he slanders me." Cao Song believed him, and after that would not hear a word the uncle brought against the boy, who ran wild at his pleasure.


Qiao Xuan, a man of that day, said to Cao Cao: "The realm will soon be in disorder, and none but a man born for such an age can save it. That man may be you." He Yong of Nanyang, after seeing him, said: "The house of Han is falling, and the man who will settle the realm is surely this one." Xu Shao of Runan was famous for his reading of men. Cao Cao went to him and asked: "What manner of man am I?" Xu Shao would not answer. Cao Cao pressed him, and Xu Shao said: "An able minister in an age of order; in an age of chaos, a treacherous hero." The words delighted Cao Cao. At twenty he was put forward as a Xiaolian, given a post at court, and appointed Northern Commandant of Luoyang. On taking office he hung ten and more cudgels painted in five colors at the four gates of the district, and whoever broke the law was beaten with them, however great his house. The uncle of the Attendant Jian Shuo was caught walking the streets at night with a blade, and Cao Cao, who was making his rounds, had him beaten like the rest. After that nobody within the walls or without dared offend, and his name was widely feared. He was afterwards made Magistrate of Dunqiu. When the Yellow Turbans rose he was given the rank of Cavalry Commandant, with five thousand horse and foot, and came to Yingchuan to join the fighting. He arrived just as Zhang Liang and Zhang Bao were in flight, and barred their way. He made a great slaughter, took more than ten thousand heads, and carried off banners, gongs, drums and horses past reckoning. The brothers fought their way clear at last. Cao Cao paid his respects to Huangfu Song and Zhu Jun, then led his troops straight off in pursuit of the two brothers.


Liu Bei meanwhile was coming up to Yingchuan with Guan Yu and Zhang Fei. They heard the noise of the killing and saw the firelight on the sky, and hurried forward, but the rebels were broken and gone before they came. Liu Bei found Huangfu Song and Zhu Jun and gave them Lu Zhi's message. "Zhang Liang and Zhang Bao are at the end of their strength," said Huangfu Song. "They will make for Guangzong and Zhang Jiao. Ride back tonight, Xuande, and help Lu Zhi."


Liu Bei took the order and turned his troops about. Halfway along the road he met a party of soldiers guarding a prison cart, and the prisoner in it was Lu Zhi. He came out of the saddle in his astonishment and asked the reason. Lu Zhi said: "I had Zhang Jiao ringed in and all but broken, but he works sorcery, so I could not beat him outright. The court sent the Yellow Gate eunuch Zuo Feng down to look into the siege. He asked me for a bribe. I told him the army was short of grain; where was I to find money to flatter the Emperor's envoy? Zuo Feng took it ill. He reported at court that I sat behind high walls, would not fight, and let the men's spirit rot. So the court in its wrath has sent the General of the Household Dong Zhuo to take over my army, and I am being taken to the capital for trial."


Zhang Fei heard it out in a great rage, and was for cutting down the escort and setting Lu Zhi free. Liu Bei stopped him. "The court has its own judgment. Would you be so rash?" The soldiers closed round Lu Zhi and took him off. "General Lu is arrested and another will have the army," said Guan Yu. "We shall have nobody to lean on there. Better go back to Zhuo for the present." Liu Bei took his advice and marched north.


They had not marched two days when a great shouting broke out behind the hills. Liu Bei rode up a high ridge with Guan Yu and Zhang Fei to look. The Han army was in full rout, and behind it, filling the hills and covering the fields, came the Yellow Turbans, with General of Heaven written large on their banners. "That is Zhang Jiao!" said Liu Bei. "We must fight at once!"


The three whipped their horses forward and brought their men out. Zhang Jiao had just beaten Dong Zhuo and was following up his advantage when the three came in upon him. His army fell into confusion and ran fifty li and more. They brought Dong Zhuo safely back to his camp, where he asked them what offices they held. "We hold no office," said Liu Bei. Dong Zhuo held them cheap and gave them no courtesy. When Liu Bei came out, Zhang Fei burst out in a fury: "We went into the bloody work ourselves and pulled the fellow out of it, and this is how he treats us! If I do not kill him, my anger will never cool." And he took up his blade and made for the tent. Truly:
    The ways of men, by gain and power led,
    Forget the hero in a common stead.
    How shall we find a Zhang Fei, fierce to slay
    All ingrate hearts upon the earth today?
Whether Dong Zhuo kept his life, the next chapter will tell.



Chapter 2
Zhang Fei in rage whips the Investigator
He Jin plots to destroy the eunuchs

Now Dong Zhuo, styled Zhongying, was a man of Lintao in Longxi, Grand Administrator of Hedong, arrogant by nature. He had slighted Liu Bei that day, and Zhang Fei, his temper up, would have cut the man down. Liu Bei and Guan Yu held him back. "The man is the court's own officer. Who are you to kill him?" Zhang Fei said: "If I do not kill the wretch, I must stay and take his orders instead, and that I cannot stomach! Stay here if you like, brothers, but I will go elsewhere alone!" "We three swore to live and die together," said Liu Bei. "How should we part? Better we all go elsewhere." "That will ease my anger a little," said Zhang Fei.

So the three marched their men through the night and joined Zhu Jun, who treated them handsomely. They put their armies together and went on against Zhang Bao. Cao Cao was at that time with Huangfu Song against Zhang Liang, and they fought a great battle at Quyang. Zhang Bao had eighty or ninety thousand men camped behind a hill. Zhu Jun made Liu Bei his vanguard and sent him against them, and Zhang Bao sent out his lieutenant Gao Sheng to offer battle. Liu Bei set Zhang Fei on him. Zhang Fei put his horse forward with his spear leveled, and they had not fought many bouts when he ran Gao Sheng through and the man fell from his horse. Liu Bei waved his men on to the charge. Then Zhang Bao, still mounted, loosed his hair, took sword in hand, and began his sorcery. Wind and thunder broke out, and a black vapor came down from the sky with what seemed an endless host riding in it. Liu Bei pulled his men back in haste, his ranks fell into confusion, and he came off the field beaten. He took counsel with Zhu Jun. "He works sorcery," said Zhu Jun. "Tomorrow we kill pigs, sheep and dogs, take their blood, and set men in hiding along the hilltop. When the rebels come on in pursuit, we pour it down from the high ground, and his spell can be broken."

On Zhu Jun's orders Liu Bei sent Guan Yu and Zhang Fei with a thousand men apiece to lie hidden on the high ridge behind the hill, with the blood of pigs, sheep and dogs and other filth made ready. Next day Zhang Bao came out with flags shaking and drums beating to offer battle, and Liu Bei rode out to meet him. As the two sides closed, Zhang Bao worked his spell. Wind and thunder broke, sand flew and stones rolled, a black vapor filled the sky, and men and horses came pouring down out of it. Liu Bei turned his horse and fled, and Zhang Bao drove his men after him. As they came over the crest the men in hiding fired the signal shot and flung the filth down all together. Paper men and straw horses came tumbling out of the air to the ground; the wind and thunder stopped at once, and no more sand flew. Zhang Bao saw the spell broken and would have drawn off, but Guan Yu came out on the left and Zhang Fei on the right, with Liu Bei and Zhu Jun close on his heels, and the rebels were routed. Liu Bei caught sight of the banner of the General of Earth and rode hard after it. Zhang Bao took to the wilds, and an arrow of Liu Bei's caught him in the left arm. He escaped with the arrow still in him, fled into Yangcheng, and shut himself up there. Zhu Jun ringed the city about and laid siege to it, and sent men for news of Huangfu Song.

The scouts came back with the whole of it. Huangfu Song had won a great victory. After Dong Zhuo's repeated defeats the court had put Huangfu Song in his place. By the time he came up Zhang Jiao was dead and Zhang Liang had the command of his people, holding out against the imperial army. Huangfu Song beat him seven times running and cut him down at Quyang. Zhang Jiao's coffin was opened, the body mutilated and the head struck off and sent to the capital, and the rest surrendered. The court had raised Huangfu Song to General of Chariots and Cavalry with the governorship of Ji Province, and he had memorialized further that Lu Zhi had merit and no fault, so that Lu Zhi was restored to his old office. Cao Cao too was rewarded, with the chancellorship of Jinan, and was to march off that same day to take it up. On this news Zhu Jun pressed his men forward and threw everything against Yangcheng. The rebels were in desperate straits, and their own officer Yan Zheng stabbed Zhang Bao, brought in his head, and surrendered. So Zhu Jun pacified several commanderies and sent up word of the victory.

Three more of the Yellow Turban remnant were up by now — Zhao Hong, Han Zhong and Sun Zhong — who had gathered tens of thousands, burned and plundered wherever they came, and claimed to be avenging Zhang Jiao. The court ordered Zhu Jun to take his victorious army against them at once. The rebels held Wancheng, and Zhu Jun brought his men up to attack it. Zhao Hong sent Han Zhong out to fight, and Zhu Jun sent Liu Bei with Guan Yu and Zhang Fei against the southwest corner. Han Zhong took the whole of his best troops to the southwest to meet them, while Zhu Jun himself loosed two thousand armored horse straight at the northeast. The rebels, afraid for the city, left the southwest in haste and turned back. Liu Bei fell on their rear and broke them, and they fled into Wancheng. Zhu Jun divided his men and ringed the place on all four sides. Grain ran out within, and Han Zhong sent a man out to offer surrender, which Zhu Jun would not have. "Gaozu won the realm because he knew how to take a surrender and welcome the submissive," said Liu Bei. "Why refuse Han Zhong?" "That was one age and this is another," said Zhu Jun. "In the days when Qin fell and Xiang Yu rose the realm was in confusion and the people had no settled master, so surrender was invited and submission rewarded, to draw men in. Now all within the seas is one, and only the Yellow Turbans are in revolt. Allow them to surrender, and nothing will be left to encourage men to good. A brigand could plunder at will while he was winning and give himself up the moment he lost. That would feed the brigand's appetite, and it is no good policy." "You are right not to take their surrender," said Liu Bei. "But we have the city ringed on every side as tight as an iron barrel. They beg to surrender and cannot, so they will fight to the death. Ten thousand men of one mind cannot be withstood, let alone the tens of thousands in there who have given their lives up for lost. Better draw off from the east and the south and attack only the northwest. They will abandon the city and run, with no heart left for fighting, and may be taken as they go."

Zhu Jun thought well of it, drew his men off the eastern and southern faces, and threw them all at the northwest. Sure enough, Han Zhong led out his men, abandoned the city and ran for it. Zhu Jun, with Liu Bei, Guan Yu and Zhang Fei, brought the whole army down on them. Han Zhong was shot dead, and the rest scattered in every direction.

They were still in pursuit when Zhao Hong and Sun Zhong came up with the rest of the rebels and gave battle. Zhu Jun saw how strong Zhao Hong was and drew off for the time, and Zhao Hong took the chance to retake Wancheng. Zhu Jun camped ten li away and was making ready to attack when a body of horse appeared out of the east. At its head rode a man with a broad brow and a wide face, a tiger's body and a bear's loins, a man of Fuchun in Wu Commandery: Sun Jian by name, Wentai by style, and descended from Sun Wu. At seventeen he had gone with his father to Qiantang and seen a dozen pirates on the bank, sharing out a merchant's goods. "These robbers can be taken," he said to his father, and went up the bank blade in hand, shouting and signaling to right and left as though calling men on. The pirates thought the government troops were upon them, dropped the whole of the plunder and ran. Sun Jian went after them and killed one. His name was known through commandery and county from that day, and he was put forward for a colonelcy. Afterwards, when the sorcerer Xu Chang of Kuaiji rebelled and styled himself the Yangming Emperor with tens of thousands at his back, Sun Jian and the commandery Major raised a thousand brave men and more, joined the provincial forces, and broke him, cutting down Xu Chang and his son Xu Shao. The Inspector Zang Min memorialized his service, and he was made Assistant Magistrate of Yandu, and after that of Xuyi and of Xiapi. Now, with the Yellow Turbans up, he had gathered the young men of his district, some merchants and travelers, and fifteen hundred picked men from the Huai and the Si, and had come to join the fighting.

Zhu Jun was delighted. He set Sun Jian on the south gate and Liu Bei on the north, took the west himself, and left the east open for the rebels to flee by. Sun Jian was first up the wall and cut down a score of the rebels, and the rest broke. Zhao Hong spurred at him with his lance leveled. Sun Jian sprang down from the wall, tore the lance from his hands and thrust him from the saddle with it, then mounted Zhao Hong's horse and rode back and forth killing rebels. Sun Zhong brought his men out by the north gate straight into Liu Bei, with no heart for fighting and nothing in mind but flight. Liu Bei drew and loosed, and the arrow took Sun Zhong and tumbled him from his horse. Zhu Jun's main body came up behind and fell on them, taking tens of thousands of heads, and those who surrendered were past counting. Throughout the Nanyang region more than ten commanderies were pacified. Zhu Jun marched back to the capital, and by edict was made General of Chariots and Cavalry and Prefect of Henan. He memorialized the services of Sun Jian, Liu Bei and the others. Sun Jian had friends at court, was given a Major's post in another commandery, and went off to take it up. But Liu Bei waited day after day and got nothing.

The three went about low in spirits, and were walking the street one day when the carriage of a court officer named Zhang Jun came by. Liu Bei stopped him and told him what he had done in the field. Zhang Jun was aghast, and went into court to see the Emperor. "The Yellow Turbans rose, and the root of it was the Ten Attendants selling offices and titles, employing none but their own and punishing none but their enemies, till the realm fell into confusion. Cut down the Ten Attendants now, hang their heads in the southern suburb, send envoys to proclaim it through the realm and reward those who have served, and all within the four seas will be quiet of itself." The Ten Attendants told the Emperor that Zhang Jun was deceiving his sovereign, and the Emperor had his guards put the man out. Then they talked it over among themselves. "This comes of the men who did good service against the Yellow Turbans and got no posts for it, and now the grudge is out. Let the ministry give them some small posts for now; there will be time enough to deal with them later." So Liu Bei was made Defender of Anxi County in Zhongshan, Dingzhou, and was to take up the post by a set day. He sent his soldiers home to their villages, kept only a score of his own people about him, and came with Guan Yu and Zhang Fei to Anxi. In a month of governing the county he had not touched so much as a hair of the people's, and they were all won over to him. From the day he took office he ate at one table with Guan Yu and Zhang Fei and slept in one bed with them. When he sat in company, the two of them stood in attendance beside him all day long and never wearied of it.

He had not been four months in the county when an edict came down that all who held office by military merit were to be weeded out, and Liu Bei feared he was among them. The Investigator came on his round to the county just then. Liu Bei went out beyond the wall to meet him and bowed. The Investigator kept his seat on his horse and answered with a slight motion of his whip, at which Guan Yu and Zhang Fei both took fire. At the posting house the Investigator took the high seat facing south, and Liu Bei stood below the steps. After a long while the Investigator asked him what his origin was. "I am descended from Prince Jing of Zhongshan," said Liu Bei. "From Zhuo Commandery onward I have cut down Yellow Turbans in thirty engagements and more, great and small, and for that small service I was given this post." The Investigator shouted at him: "You lie about your imperial kindred and invent your services! The court's edict has just come down to weed out corrupt and worthless officers like you!" Liu Bei murmured assent and withdrew. Back in the county he took counsel with his clerks. "The Investigator throws his weight about for nothing but a bribe," said a clerk. "I have not touched a hair of the people's," said Liu Bei. "Where am I to find money for him?" Next day the Investigator began by seizing the county clerks, and tried to force them to swear that the Defender had oppressed the people. Liu Bei went several times himself to plead for their release, but the gatemen turned him back every time.

Now Zhang Fei had drunk a few cups to drown his gloom, and was riding past the posting house when he saw fifty or sixty old men at the gate weeping. He asked what was the matter, and they answered: "The Investigator is forcing the clerks to ruin Lord Liu. We came to plead for him, but they would not let us in, and the gatemen beat us and drove us off!" A great rage took Zhang Fei. His round eyes stared, he ground his teeth, and he was out of the saddle and into the posting house before the gatemen could stop him. He went straight through to the back, where the Investigator sat in the hall with the county clerks bound on the floor. "Robber of the people!" Zhang Fei roared at him. "Do you know who I am?" Before the Investigator could get a word out Zhang Fei had him by the hair, hauled him out of the posting house and up to the hitching post before the county office, tied him fast to it, broke off a willow switch, and laid it on across both his legs with a will, breaking a dozen switches one after another.

Liu Bei was still brooding when he heard the uproar in front of the office and asked what it was. "General Zhang has a man tied up before the office and is beating him." He hurried out to look, and the man tied there was the Investigator. He asked in alarm what had happened. "A robber of the people like this deserves to be beaten to death!" said Zhang Fei. "Lord Xuande, save my life!" cried the Investigator. Liu Bei was a merciful man after all, and called sharply to Zhang Fei to stop. Guan Yu came round from the side. "Brother, you have won great merit again and again and have only a county Defender's post for it, and now an Investigator insults you. A thicket of thorns is no place for a phoenix to roost. Kill the man, I say, give up the office and go home, and look to something larger." So Liu Bei took the seal and ribbon, hung them about the Investigator's neck, and rebuked him: "For the harm you have done the people you have earned death, but for this once I spare you. Here are the seal and ribbon. From this hour I am gone!" The Investigator reported to the Grand Administrator of Dingzhou, who sent a statement up to the provincial office, and officers went out to arrest them. The three went to Daizhou and took refuge with Liu Hui, who, seeing that Liu Bei was of the imperial house, kept them hidden in his own home.

The Ten Attendants, with the power now in their hands, agreed among themselves that any man who would not go with them should be destroyed. Zhao Zhong and Zhang Rang sent to the officers who had broken the Yellow Turbans to demand gold and silk, and memorialized the dismissal of all who refused. Huangfu Song and Zhu Jun would give nothing, and were stripped of office for it. The Emperor also made Zhao Zhong General of Chariots and Cavalry, and Zhang Rang and twelve more were made full marquises. Government rotted further and the people murmured. Then the rebel Ou Xing rose in Changsha, and Zhang Ju and Zhang Chun rebelled in Yuyang, the one calling himself Son of Heaven and the other Grand General. Urgent memorials came thick as snow, and the Ten Attendants hid every one of them and reported nothing.

One day the Emperor was drinking with the Ten Attendants in the back garden when Liu Tao, Counselor Remonstrant, came straight up before him and broke into weeping. The Emperor asked the cause. "The realm may be lost within a day, and Your Majesty sits drinking with eunuchs!" said Liu Tao. "The country is at peace. Where is the danger?" "Brigands are up on every side and overrunning the provinces, and the whole of it comes of the Ten Attendants selling office, preying on the people and deceiving their sovereign. Every honest man has left the court. The ruin is before your eyes!" The Ten Attendants took off their caps and threw themselves down before the Emperor. "If the great ministers will not have us, we cannot live. We beg leave to go home to our fields with our lives, and we will give up our whole estates to help pay for the army." And they wept bitterly. The Emperor turned on Liu Tao in anger. "You have your own attendants about you. Why will you not let me have mine?" And he called the guards to take the man out and behead him. Liu Tao cried aloud as they took him: "I do not grudge my death! I grieve for the house of Han — four hundred years and more, and all of it ended in a morning!"

The guards hustled Liu Tao out and were about to strike when one of the great ministers called to them to stop. "Hold your hands until I have pleaded with His Majesty." It was Chen Dan, Minister of the Masses, and he went straight in to the Emperor. "What is Counselor Liu's crime, that he is to be put to death?" "He slandered my attendants and affronted my person." Chen Dan said: "The people of the realm would eat the flesh of the Ten Attendants, and Your Majesty honors them as father and mother. They have not an inch of merit among them, and every one of them is made a full marquis. Feng Xu besides was in league with the Yellow Turbans and would have risen inside the palace. If Your Majesty does not reflect now, the altars of the land will come down before your eyes!" "Feng Xu's treason was never proved," said the Emperor. "Are there not one or two loyal men among the Ten Attendants?" Chen Dan beat his head against the steps as he pleaded. The Emperor in anger had him dragged away, and he and Liu Tao were both put in prison, where the Ten Attendants murdered them that same night. Then they forged an edict making Sun Jian Grand Administrator of Changsha, to put down Ou Xing.

In under fifty days word came that the victory was won and Jiangxia pacified. Sun Jian was made Marquis of Wucheng by edict, and Liu Yu was made Governor of You Province and sent with troops to Yuyang against Zhang Ju and Zhang Chun. Liu Hui of Daizhou recommended Liu Bei to Liu Yu by letter. Liu Yu was greatly pleased and made him a Commandant, and Liu Bei led his men straight to the rebels' nest, fought them for several days on end, and broke their spirit. Zhang Chun was cruel and violent through and through, and his soldiers turned against him. One of his own officers stabbed him, brought in the head, and came over with the men under him. Zhang Ju, seeing all was lost, hanged himself, and the whole of Yuyang was subdued. Liu Yu memorialized Liu Bei's great service, and the court pardoned him the whipping of the Investigator, made him Assistant Magistrate of Xiami, and then promoted him Defender of Gaotang. Gongsun Zan also sent up a memorial setting out Liu Bei's earlier services, and recommended him as Major of a Detached Division and acting Magistrate of Pingyuan County. At Pingyuan Liu Bei had money, grain and troops enough, and built up his old standing again. Liu Yu, for his part in putting down the rebels, was made Grand Commandant.

In the sixth year of Zhongping, the fourth month of summer, the Emperor lay gravely ill, and called the Grand General He Jin into the palace to settle what should come after. He Jin had risen out of a butcher's household. His sister had gone into the palace as a Noble Lady, borne a son, Bian, and been made Empress, and from this came his own power and high office. The Emperor had also favored Lady Wang, who bore him a son, Xie. Empress He poisoned her out of jealousy, and the boy was brought up in the palace of Empress Dowager Dong. The Empress Dowager was the Emperor's own mother, wife of Liu Chang, Marquis of Jiedu. Emperor Huan had died without a son, and Liu Chang's son was brought in and set on the throne as Emperor Ling. On coming to the throne he brought his mother to live in the palace and honored her as Empress Dowager.

Empress Dowager Dong had once urged the Emperor to make Prince Xie heir apparent, and he too doted on the boy and meant to name him. Now, as he lay gravely ill, the Attendant Jian Shuo urged him: "If Xie is to be set up, He Jin must be killed first, to cut off future trouble." The Emperor thought well of it, and summoned He Jin to the palace. He Jin came as far as the palace gate, where Major Pan Yin said to him: "You must not go in. Jian Shuo means to murder you." He Jin took fright, hurried back to his own house, and called the great ministers together, meaning to destroy the eunuchs root and branch. One of those sitting there rose. "The power of the eunuchs goes back to the reigns of Emperors Chong and Zhi, and it has spread so wide through the court that it cannot all be cut away. If the secret leaks, your whole clan will be destroyed. I beg you to consider it closely." He Jin looked, and it was Cao Cao, a Colonel. "What does a stripling like you know of the great affairs of state?" he said sharply.

He Jin was still hesitating when Pan Yin came in. "The Emperor is dead. Jian Shuo and the Ten Attendants have agreed to keep the death secret and send out a forged summons calling Imperial In-law He into the palace, to cut off future trouble, and then set the prince Xie on the throne."

He had not finished when the summons came, calling He Jin in at once to settle the succession. Cao Cao said: "Today we must first set the succession right, and deal with the traitors after." "Who will stand with me to set up the rightful lord and put down the traitors?" said He Jin. A man stepped out. "Give me five thousand picked men. I will cut my way in through the gates, set up the new sovereign, destroy the eunuchs to the last of them, sweep the court clean, and give the realm peace!" He Jin looked, and it was the son of Yuan Feng, Minister of the Masses, and nephew of Yuan Wei: Yuan Shao by name, Benchu by style, at that time Director of Retainers. He Jin was delighted, and mustered five thousand of the Imperial Guard. Yuan Shao put on full armor, and He Jin led in more than thirty great officers, He Yong, Xun You and Zheng Tai among them, one after another. Before Emperor Ling's coffin they raised the heir apparent Bian to the throne.

When the officials had made their acclamation and their bows, Yuan Shao went into the palace to take Jian Shuo. Jian Shuo fled in a panic into the imperial garden, where the Attendant Guo Sheng killed him in the shade of the flowers. The guards Jian Shuo had commanded all came over. "The eunuchs are a faction," said Yuan Shao to He Jin. "The chance is here today. Destroy them all." Zhang Rang and the others saw their danger and hurried in to Empress Dowager He. "It was Jian Shuo alone who laid the plot against the Grand General at the beginning, and no business of ours. Now the Grand General listens to Yuan Shao and would destroy us all. We beg Your Majesty's pity!" "Have no fear. I will protect you." She sent out word summoning He Jin, and spoke to him privately. "You and I came of poor and humble stock. Without Zhang Rang and the rest, how could we have come to this wealth and honor? Jian Shuo was the wicked one, and he has paid for it. Why do you listen to others and want every eunuch killed?"

When she had finished, He Jin went out and told the officers: "Jian Shuo plotted against my life, and his family shall be destroyed to the last of them. There is no need to harm the rest." Yuan Shao said: "If the grass is cut and the root left, it will be the death of you." "My mind is made up. No more of it." And they all withdrew.

Next day the Empress Dowager made He Jin joint head of the Secretariat and gave office to all the rest. Empress Dowager Dong called Zhang Rang and the others in to take counsel. "It was I who raised He Jin's sister up in the beginning. Today her child sits on the throne, and every officer within the palace and without is of the He family's party. Their power is too great. What am I to do?" Zhang Rang said: "Your Majesty may hold court and govern from behind the screen. Raise the prince Xie to a princedom, give the Imperial In-law Dong Zhong high office and the command of troops, and put us in places of trust. Then the great design can be carried through."

Empress Dowager Dong was delighted. Next day she held court and issued a decree making Xie Prince of Chenliu and Dong Zhong General of Agile Cavalry, and giving Zhang Rang and the rest a share in the government. Empress Dowager He, seeing Empress Dowager Dong take all power into her own hands, set out a feast in the palace and invited her to it. When the wine was half done she rose, lifted her cup, bowed twice, and said: "We are both of us women. It is not fitting that we should meddle in government. In the old days Empress Lü held great power, and a thousand of her clan were put to the sword for it. We should keep to the deep seclusion of the palace and leave the great affairs of the court to the ministers and elders to settle, for the state's good. I beg you to hear me." Empress Dowager Dong said in a fury: "You poisoned Lady Wang out of a jealous heart. Now you lean on your son's throne and your brother He Jin's power, and dare to speak out of turn! I have only to order the General of Agile Cavalry to take your brother's head off, and it is done as easily as turning over a hand!" Empress Dowager He, angry in her turn, said: "I gave you good counsel. Why round on me for it?" "Butchers' and wine sellers' brats! What understanding should you people have?"

The two Empresses Dowager fell to quarreling, and Zhang Rang and the rest persuaded them each back to her own apartments. That night Empress Dowager He called He Jin in and told him what had passed. He Jin went out and called the Three Ducal Ministers into council. At the morning court the officers were to memorialize that Empress Dowager Dong, being by origin the consort of a provincial marquis, ought not to live on in the palace, but should be moved to Hejian and settled there, and should be out of the capital that same day. Men were sent to start her on the road; at the same time palace guards surrounded the house of Dong Zhong, General of Agile Cavalry, and demanded his seal of office. Dong Zhong saw how it stood and cut his own throat in the back hall. His household raised the wailing for him, and only then did the soldiers draw off. Zhang Rang and Duan Gui, seeing the Dong branch broken, set about winning over He Jin's younger brother He Miao and his mother, the Lady of Wuyang, with gifts of gold, pearls and curiosities, so that they would go in to the Empress Dowager morning and evening and cover for them with fair words. So the Ten Attendants came back into favor.

In the sixth month He Jin had Empress Dowager Dong poisoned secretly at the posting house at Hejian, brought her coffin back to the capital, and buried her at Wenling. He then pleaded sickness and kept indoors. Yuan Shao, Director of Retainers, came in to see him. "Zhang Rang and Duan Gui have put it about that you poisoned the Empress Dowager and are plotting to seize power. Destroy the eunuchs now, or great harm will come of it later. Dou Wu meant to destroy them once; his plan leaked, and the ruin fell on him instead. Today the officers and troops under you and your brother are all able men, and if you set them to it the work is as good as done. Heaven is giving you the moment, and it must not be let slip." "Let me think it over," said He Jin. One of those about him reported it privately to Zhang Rang, who passed it to He Miao with more bribes, and He Miao went in to Empress Dowager He. "The Grand General, who should be supporting the new sovereign, shows no mercy and thinks of killing only. Now he would kill the Ten Attendants too, with no cause at all. This is the road to disorder." And she listened to him.

Not long after, He Jin came in to tell the Empress Dowager that he meant to destroy the eunuchs. "It is the old usage of Han that the eunuchs have charge of the palace," she said. "The late Emperor has only just left the world, and you would put the old servants to death. That is no way to honor the ancestral temple." He Jin was by nature irresolute; he heard her out, said yes, and came away. Yuan Shao met him and asked how the great business stood. "The Empress Dowager will not have it. What is to be done?" "Summon the bold men of the provinces and bring their troops to the capital, and destroy the eunuchs to the last. With matters so urgent, the Empress Dowager will not be able to refuse." "An excellent plan!" said He Jin, and sent out the call to the garrisons to come up to the capital.

Chen Lin the Registrar spoke against it. "It will not do! The proverb says that to cover your eyes and catch sparrows is to cheat yourself. If even so small a creature cannot be caught by deceit, how much less the great business of the state? General, you have the imperial authority behind you and the forces in your hand; you have a dragon's majesty and a tiger's stride, and may raise or cast down whom you will. To destroy the eunuchs would be no more than fanning a great furnace to singe a hair. You have only to strike quickly, use your power and decide at once, and Heaven and men will be with you. But to send out a call and bring the great men of the provinces up against the gates of the capital, where the bold will gather, each with a mind of his own, is what they call holding a sword by the blade and offering another the hilt. It cannot succeed, and will breed disorder instead." He Jin laughed. "A coward's view!" Then a man at his side clapped his hands and laughed aloud. "It is as easy as turning over a hand. Why so much talk?" It was Cao Cao. Truly:
    Would he sweep out the knaves that vex the throne,
    He must heed counsel wiser than his own.
What Cao Cao had to say, the next chapter will tell.

Cao Cao said to He Jin that day: "The mischief of eunuchs is no new thing. But rulers should never have lent them power and favor, and let it come to this. If they are to be punished, cut off the chief offender, and one jailer is enough for the work. What need is there to call up all these troops from outside? Try to kill them to the last man and the design is sure to get out. I say it must fail." "Does Mengde too harbor some purpose of his own?" said He Jin angrily. As he withdrew Cao Cao said: "The man who throws the realm into disorder will be He Jin." So He Jin sent his messengers out in secret with the sealed edicts, riding through the night to the garrisons.


Dong Zhuo was at this time General of the Vanguard, Marquis of Aoxiang and Inspector of Xiliang. He had won nothing against the Yellow Turbans, and the court would have called him to account for it, but a bribe to the Ten Attendants saved him; afterwards he attached himself to the great men of the court, rose to high office, and commanded an army of two hundred thousand in the western provinces, with a disloyal heart in him all the while. The edict came, to his delight. He mustered his men and sent them off division by division, left his son-in-law Niu Fu, a General of the Household, to hold Shaanxi, and marched on Luoyang himself with Li Jue, Guo Si, Zhang Ji and Fan Chou. Dong Zhuo's son-in-law and counselor Li Ru said: "We have the edict, but there is much in it that is not above board. Send up a memorial. Then we come openly and with good warrant, and the great design may be carried through." Dong Zhuo was well pleased, and sent one up. The substance of it ran: "Your servant has heard that rebellion in the realm never ceases, and that the whole of it comes of Zhang Rang and the Yellow Gate Attendants, who flout the constant law of Heaven. It is said that to stop a pot boiling it is better to take away the firewood than to ladle the water, and that to lance an abscess, though it hurt, is better than to nurse the poison. Your servant makes bold to enter Luoyang with bell and drum and rid the court of Zhang Rang and his fellows, to the great good of the altars and the great good of the realm!"


He Jin received the memorial and showed it to the ministers. Zheng Tai, an Imperial Clerk, spoke against it: "Dong Zhuo is a wolf. Bring him into the capital and he will eat men." He Jin said: "You have too many doubts for great designs." Lu Zhi urged the same. "I know Dong Zhuo of old — a kindly face and a savage heart. Let him once inside the palace and there will be mischief for certain. Better stop him from coming at all, and spare ourselves the disorder."


He Jin would not listen, and Zheng Tai and Lu Zhi both gave up their offices and went, with more than half the great men of the court after them. He Jin sent to meet Dong Zhuo at Mianchi, and there Dong Zhuo halted his troops and did not stir. Zhang Rang and the rest heard that the provincial troops had come. "This is He Jin's doing," they said among themselves. "Strike first, or our clans will be wiped out." So they laid fifty men with swords and axes in hiding inside the Jiade Gate of the Changle Palace, and went in to Empress Dowager He. "The Grand General has forged an edict and called provincial troops up to the capital to destroy us. We look to Your Majesty's pity to save us." "Go to the Grand General's residence and ask his pardon," said the Empress Dowager. Zhang Rang said: "If we go to his house we shall be ground to dust, flesh and bone together. We beg Your Majesty to summon the Grand General into the palace and bid him let the matter drop. If he will not hear you, let us die here in your presence."


So the Empress Dowager sent out her summons, and He Jin set off the moment it came. Chen Lin the Registrar spoke against it: "This summons of the Empress Dowager's is the Ten Attendants' work for certain. You must not go. Nothing but harm can come of it." "The Empress Dowager summons me. What harm should there be?" said He Jin. Yuan Shao said: "Now that the plot is out and the matter known, General, would you still walk into the palace?" "Call the Ten Attendants out first, and then you may go in," said Cao Cao. He Jin laughed. "A child's view. I hold the power of the realm in my hand. What dare the Ten Attendants do?" "If you are set on going, we will bring armed men as escort, against anything unlooked for," said Yuan Shao.


So Yuan Shao and Cao Cao each picked five hundred of their best men and gave the command of them to Yuan Shao's brother Yuan Shu. Yuan Shu put on full armor and drew the men up outside the Qingsuo Gate, while Yuan Shao and Cao Cao, swords at their sides, brought He Jin as far as the front of the Changle Palace. There a Yellow Gate eunuch came out with the Empress Dowager's word: "Her Majesty summons the Grand General alone. No one else may come in." Yuan Shao, Cao Cao and the rest were held at the palace gate, and He Jin walked in with his head high. At the door of the Jiade Hall Zhang Rang and Duan Gui came out to meet him, their men closed round him on both sides, and a great fear took He Jin. Zhang Rang rebuked him in a hard voice: "What was Empress Dong's crime, that she should be poisoned out of the way? When the mother of the realm was buried, you pleaded sickness and would not come! You are a butcher's brat, and it was we who commended you to the Son of Heaven and made your fortune, yet instead of repaying it you plot our deaths! You call us foul; who then is clean?" In a panic He Jin looked for a way out, but every gate was shut, and the men in hiding came out together and hacked him in two. A later poet sighed over him:
    The house of Han was sinking to its fate,
    And witless He Jin sat among the great.
    Oft warned by loyal men, he scorned them all,
    And took the sword's edge in the palace hall.


When Zhang Rang and the rest had killed He Jin, Yuan Shao waited a long while outside and saw nothing of him, and called out at the palace gate: "Your carriage is waiting, General!" For answer they threw He Jin's head over the wall and proclaimed: "He Jin plotted treason and has been put to death. The rest of you were forced into his following and are pardoned." Yuan Shao raised a great shout: "The eunuchs have murdered a great minister! Let whoever would kill the wicked crew come out and fight!" Then Wu Kuang, one of He Jin's officers, set fire to the Qingsuo Gate from outside, and Yuan Shu broke into the palace courts with his men and killed every eunuch he found, high or low. Yuan Shao and Cao Cao hacked the gate open and went in after them. Zhao Zhong, Cheng Kuang, Xia Yun and Guo Sheng were run down in front of the Cuihua Tower and chopped to a pulp. The flames in the palace went up to heaven. Zhang Rang, Duan Gui, Cao Jie and Hou Lan carried off the Empress Dowager, the young Emperor and the Prince of Chenliu to the inner apartments, and fled by the back way to the North Palace.


Lu Zhi had given up his office but had not yet left the capital. Seeing the palace in uproar, he put on his mail, took a dagger-axe and stood below the gallery. From a distance he saw Duan Gui hustling the Empress Dowager along, and roared out: "Traitor Duan Gui! How dare you carry off the Empress Dowager!" Duan Gui turned and ran. The Empress Dowager jumped from a window, and Lu Zhi reached her in time and brought her off safe. Wu Kuang fought his way into the inner court and found He Miao coming out with a drawn sword. "He Miao had a hand in his own brother's murder," he shouted. "Let us kill him together!" "Cut the traitor down!" the men answered with one voice. He Miao turned to run, but they ringed him round and cut him to pieces. Yuan Shao then sent soldiers out in parties to kill the households of the Ten Attendants, old and young alike, and not one was left alive; many a beardless man was killed in error. Cao Cao meanwhile put out the fires in the palace, begged the Empress Dowager to take the government in hand for the time, sent troops after Zhang Rang, and set men to look for the young Emperor.


Zhang Rang and Duan Gui, meanwhile, had carried the young Emperor and the Prince of Chenliu off through the smoke and fire, and ran with them all night as far as Mount Beimang. About the third watch a great shouting rose behind them and horse and foot came up, with Min Gong, an officer of the central division of Henan, at their head, crying: "Halt, traitors!" Zhang Rang saw there was no escape, and threw himself into the river and drowned. The Emperor and the Prince of Chenliu did not know what was behind the noise, and dared not call out, but lay hidden in the rough grass of the bank. The horsemen scattered to search, but did not find where the Emperor was.


The Emperor and the Prince of Chenliu lay there till the fourth watch. The dew came down, they were faint with hunger, and they clung to one another and wept, swallowing their sobs in the grass for fear of being heard. "We cannot stay here long," said the Prince of Chenliu. "We must find some other way out." So the two knotted their clothes together and climbed the bank. Thorns grew thick on every side, and in the dark no road showed. They were at their wits' end when of a sudden a swarm of fireflies came up in their hundreds, and their light went circling before the Emperor. "Heaven is helping us two brothers!" said the Prince of Chenliu. They followed the fireflies, and little by little a path appeared. By the fifth watch their feet were too sore to carry them further. Beside the hill was a heap of straw, and the two lay down at the foot of it. In front of the heap stood a farm. That night its master had dreamed of two red suns falling behind his house; he woke in alarm, threw on his clothes and went out to look about him, and saw a red light shooting up to the sky above the straw heap behind the farm. He hurried over, and there they were, lying beside the straw.


"Whose sons are you two boys?" asked the master of the farm. The Emperor dared not answer. The Prince of Chenliu pointed to him. "This is the Emperor who reigns today. He fled here out of the disorder the Ten Attendants have made. I am his brother, the Prince of Chenliu." The man was astonished, and bowed twice. "I am Cui Yi, brother of Cui Lie, who was Minister of the Masses in the last reign. I have lived hidden here since I saw the Ten Attendants selling offices and hounding good men." He helped the Emperor into the house and knelt to set food and wine before him.


Min Gong, meanwhile, had overtaken Duan Gui and seized him, and asked where the Son of Heaven was. Duan Gui said they had been separated on the road, and he did not know where the Emperor had gone. So Min Gong killed him, hung the head at his horse's neck, divided his men to search in every direction, and rode on alone along the road. He came by chance to Cui Yi's farm. Cui Yi saw the head and asked about it, and Min Gong told him the whole story. Cui Yi brought him in to the Emperor, and sovereign and servant wept together. "The country cannot be a single day without its lord," said Min Gong. "I beg Your Majesty to return to the capital." There was only one lean horse on the farm, and they saddled it for the Emperor. Min Gong and the Prince of Chenliu rode together on another. They had not gone three li from the farm when Wang Yun, Minister of the Masses, Yang Biao, Grand Commandant, Chunyu Qiong, Colonel of the Left Army, Zhao Meng, Colonel of the Right Army, Bao Xin, Colonel of the Rear Army, and Yuan Shao, Colonel of the Central Army, came up with several hundred men and horse and met the imperial party. There was weeping on all sides. Duan Gui's head was sent ahead to be shown in the capital, good horses were brought for the Emperor and the Prince of Chenliu, and they rode back to the city with a press of men about them. There had been a rhyme among the children of Luoyang before this:
    Emperor is emperor none,
    Prince is prince no more;
    Car on car, horse on horse, to Beimang pour.
Thus was the prophecy fulfilled.


They had not gone many li more when banners darkened the sun and dust hid the sky, and a body of troops came up. The officials turned pale, and the Emperor was badly frightened. Yuan Shao spurred out and asked who they were. Out from the shadow of the embroidered flags flew a general, who called in a hard voice: "Where is the Son of Heaven?" The Emperor shook and could not speak. The Prince of Chenliu reined his horse forward. "Who comes there?" he said sharply. "Dong Zhuo, Inspector of Xiliang." "Have you come to guard the imperial carriage, or to seize it?" "I have come to guard it." "Then since you come to guard it, and the Son of Heaven is here, why are you not off your horse?" Dong Zhuo was taken aback. He scrambled down in haste and made his bow at the left of the road. The Prince of Chenliu spoke to him and put him at his ease, and from first to last there was not a word out of place. Dong Zhuo wondered at him privately, and from that hour he had it in mind to set another on the throne.


That day they came back to the palace and saw Empress Dowager He, and all of them wept bitterly. When they took stock of the palace, the Heirloom Seal of the Realm was missing. Dong Zhuo camped his army outside the walls and came into the city every day with his mailed horsemen, riding through the streets as he pleased, and the people lived in dread. He went in and out of the palace itself with hardly a show of deference. Bao Xin, Colonel of the Rear Army, came to Yuan Shao and told him that Dong Zhuo certainly meant mischief and should be removed at once. "The court is only just settled," said Yuan Shao. "We must not move lightly." Bao Xin went to Wang Yun and said the same. "Let it wait till we have talked it over," said Wang Yun. So Bao Xin took his own troops and went off to Mount Tai.


Dong Zhuo drew over the troops that had served He Jin and his brother, until the whole of them were in his hand. "I have a mind to put down the Emperor and set up the Prince of Chenliu," he said privately to Li Ru. "What do you say?" "The court is without a master," said Li Ru. "If it is not done now, delay will breed trouble. Tomorrow call the officials together in the Wenming Garden and tell them the Emperor is to be changed. Cut down whoever will not have it. Today is the day to make your authority good."


Dong Zhuo was pleased. Next day he laid out a grand feast and invited the great officers of the whole court, and not one of them dared stay away, for they all feared him. He waited until the officials were assembled, then rode slowly up to the garden gate, dismounted, and walked to his place with his sword on. When the wine had gone round several times he had it stopped and the music silenced, and said in a hard voice: "I have something to say. Let the officials hear it in silence." Every man turned his ear. Dong Zhuo said: "The Son of Heaven is lord of all the people, and without presence and dignity he cannot serve the ancestral temple and the altars of the land. The Emperor who now reigns is a weakling. He is not the equal of the Prince of Chenliu, who is quick and fond of his books and fit to take the great seat. I mean to put down the Emperor and set up the Prince of Chenliu. What do you ministers say?" Not one dared make a sound. Then a man pushed back his table, strode out before the feast and cried: "It shall not be! Who are you, that you dare talk so high? The Son of Heaven is the late Emperor's lawful son and has done no wrong. What right have you to talk of putting down an emperor? Would you be a usurper?" Dong Zhuo looked, and it was Ding Yuan, Inspector of Jing Province. "Who follows me lives; who crosses me dies!" Dong Zhuo roared at him, and drew his sword to cut Ding Yuan down.


Just then Li Ru noticed a man standing behind Ding Yuan, of lofty bearing and commanding presence, holding a crescent-bladed halberd, his eyes blazing. Li Ru stepped up quickly. "This is a drinking party, and no place to talk of state affairs. Tomorrow at the hall of state will be soon enough to debate it in the open." The company all urged Ding Yuan to his horse, and he rode away. Dong Zhuo turned to the officials. "Have I not spoken justly?" Lu Zhi answered: "You are wrong, Your Excellency. In the old days Tai Jia was unfit to rule, and Yi Yin banished him to the Tong palace. The Prince of Changyi had been twenty-seven days on the throne and had done above three thousand wicked things, so Huo Guang announced it in the ancestral temple and put him down. The Emperor who now reigns is young, but he is quick and humane and wise, and has not a hair's weight of fault in him. You are an Inspector of an outer commandery, have had no share in the government, and have none of the great gifts of Yi Yin or Huo Guang. By what right would you force through a change of emperors? The sage says: 'With Yi Yin's purpose it may be done; without Yi Yin's purpose it is usurpation.'" Dong Zhuo in a great rage drew his sword and came at him to kill him. Peng Bo, a Court Counselor, spoke up: "Lu Zhi of the Secretariat is looked up to throughout the land. Cut him down first, and I fear the whole realm will take fright." At that Dong Zhuo held his hand. Wang Yun, Minister of the Masses, said: "A matter of deposing and enthroning is not to be settled over wine. Let us debate it another day." So the officials all dispersed. Dong Zhuo stood at the garden gate with his hand on his sword, and saw a man wheeling his horse up and down outside, a halberd in his hand. He asked Li Ru who it was. "That is Ding Yuan's foster son," said Li Ru. "Lü is his surname, Bu his name, Fengxian his style. You had better keep out of his way, my lord." So Dong Zhuo went into the garden out of sight.


Next day word came that Ding Yuan had brought his army outside the walls and offered battle. Dong Zhuo in anger led his own men out with Li Ru to meet him. The two lines drew up face to face, and there was Lü Bu with a gold coronet binding his hair, a battle robe figured with a hundred flowers, a coat of beast-hide mail and a jeweled belt with lions worked upon it. He put his horse forward with his halberd leveled and rode out before the line at Ding Yuan's side. Ding Yuan pointed at Dong Zhuo and cursed him: "The country is in misfortune, the eunuchs have played with power, and the people are in the mire for it. You have not an inch of merit to your name. How dare you talk idly of putting down an emperor and throwing the court into confusion?"


Before Dong Zhuo could answer, Lü Bu flew at him on his horse. Dong Zhuo turned and ran, Ding Yuan brought his army on after him, and Dong Zhuo's men were badly beaten. He fell back more than thirty li, made his camp and called his people together. "Lü Bu is no ordinary man," he said. "If I had him, what trouble could the realm give me?" A man stepped forward from before the tent. "My lord, set your mind at rest. Lü Bu and I are from the same village, and I know him for a brave man without judgment, who forgets his honor at the sight of a profit. My tongue shall bring him in with folded hands. What do you say to it?"


Dong Zhuo was delighted. He looked, and it was Li Su, an officer of the Tiger Guards. "How will you work on him?" "I hear you have a famous horse, my lord, called Red Hare, that goes a thousand li in a day," said Li Su. "Let me have the horse, and gold and pearls besides, to bind his heart with profit. Then I will put my arguments to him, and Lü Bu will turn against Ding Yuan and come over to you for certain." Dong Zhuo asked Li Ru whether the plan would serve. "You would have the realm, my lord," said Li Ru. "What is one horse to grudge?" Dong Zhuo gave the horse gladly, and a thousand liang of gold with it, several dozen bright pearls, and a jade belt.


Li Su took the presents and rode to Lü Bu's camp, where the pickets closed round him. "Tell General Lü quickly that an old friend has come to see him," he said. The soldiers reported it, and Lü Bu had him brought in. "Brother, I trust you have been well since we parted!" said Li Su. Lü Bu bowed. "It is long since we met. Where are you now?" "I hold the post of General of the Tiger Guards. I was glad past telling to hear that you are upholding the altars of the land. I have a good horse that goes a thousand li in a day and crosses water and climbs hills as on level ground. He is called Red Hare, and I have brought him as a gift to you, brother, to aid a tiger's strength." Lü Bu had the horse led up for him to see. Red he was from head to foot, the red of burning coal, without a hair of another color on him, a zhang long from head to tail and eight chi high from hoof to neck. When he neighed and roared he seemed ready to mount the sky or plunge into the sea. A later poet wrote of Red Hare:
    A thousand li at speed, and dust-clouds fly;
    Through flood and hill the purple mists roll by.
    The silken rein is snapped, the jade bit shakes;
    A fire-dragon from the ninth heaven breaks.


Lü Bu was overjoyed with the horse and thanked Li Su. "Brother, you give me such a steed. How am I to repay it?" "I came for friendship's sake," said Li Su. "What repayment should I seek?" Lü Bu set wine before him. When the wine was in them Li Su said: "You and I see little of each other, brother, but I meet your father often enough." "You are drunk!" said Lü Bu. "My father has been many years out of the world. How should you be meeting him?" Li Su laughed aloud. "Not he. I mean your father of today, Inspector Ding." Lü Bu looked abashed. "I am with Ding Jianyang because I have no choice." "You have it in you to hold up the sky and bridle the sea, brother," said Li Su. "Who within the four seas does not look up to you? Rank and fame and riches are in your reach, as easy as putting your hand in a bag. Why talk of having no choice, and stand under another man?" "I have not found my master, that is all," said Lü Bu. Li Su smiled. "A good bird chooses its tree to roost in, and a wise servant chooses his lord to serve. Be slow to see your chance, and repentance will come too late." "You are at court," said Lü Bu. "Who do you see there that is the hero of the age?" Li Su answered: "I have looked them all over, and there is not one the equal of Dong Zhuo. He honors the worthy and is courteous to men of talent, his rewards and punishments are clear, and he will come to great things in the end." "I would follow him," said Lü Bu, "but I have no way to reach him."


Li Su brought out the gold, the pearls and the jade belt and laid them before him. Lü Bu was startled. "What is all this for?" Li Su had the attendants dismissed. "Lord Dong has long admired your great name, and sent me with these. The Red Hare is his gift too." "If Lord Dong holds me so dear, how am I to repay him?" said Lü Bu. "A man of small ability like myself is a General of the Tiger Guards," said Li Su. "If you go to him there is no saying how high you will stand." "Yet I have not a grain of merit to bring him as a present on first meeting." "The merit is there for the turning of a hand, if only you are willing." Lü Bu sat a long while over it. "I have a mind to kill Ding Yuan and bring my men over to Dong Zhuo. What do you say?" Li Su said: "If you will, brother, there can be no greater service! But the business will not bear delay. Settle it quickly."


Lü Bu promised Li Su to come over the next day, and Li Su took his leave. That night about the second watch Lü Bu walked into Ding Yuan's tent with a blade in his hand. Ding Yuan was reading by candlelight, and looked up as he came in. "My son, what brings you here?" "I am a man in my own right," said Lü Bu. "Why should I be your son?" "Fengxian, what has changed your heart?" Lü Bu went forward and took Ding Yuan's head off at a stroke, then shouted to those about him: "I have killed Ding Yuan for his cruelty! Let those who will follow me stay. Those who will not may go!" More than half the soldiers dispersed.


Next day Lü Bu carried Ding Yuan's head to Li Su, who took him in to Dong Zhuo. Dong Zhuo was overjoyed and set wine before him, and bowed to him first. "General, to have you today is like a parched field getting sweet rain." Lü Bu seated him and made his own bow. "If you will not refuse me, let me do you reverence as my foster father." Dong Zhuo gave him golden armor and a brocade robe, and they drank deep and parted. From that day Dong Zhuo's power and prestige grew greater still. He took the office of General of the Vanguard upon himself, made his brother Dong Min General of the Left and Marquis of Hu, and made Lü Bu Cavalry Commandant, General of the Household and Marquis of the Capital Village. Li Ru pressed Dong Zhuo to settle the matter of the deposition quickly, so he set out a feast in the palace, gathered the great officers, and had Lü Bu stand guard at his side with more than a thousand men in mail.


That day Yuan Wei, the Grand Tutor, came with the whole body of officials. When the wine had gone round a few times Dong Zhuo drew his sword. "The Emperor who now reigns is dull and weak, unfit to serve the ancestral temple. I shall do as Yi Yin and Huo Guang did before me: put him down to be Prince of Hongnong and set up the Prince of Chenliu as Emperor. Whoever will not have it dies!" The ministers were terrified, and not one dared answer. Then Yuan Shao, Colonel of the Central Army, stepped straight out. "The Emperor has not been long on the throne and has done nothing to forfeit it. You would put down the son of the Empress and set up the son of a lesser consort. What is that but treason?" Dong Zhuo said in a rage: "The business of the realm lies with me! Who dares refuse? Do you think my sword not sharp?" Yuan Shao drew his own. "Your sword is sharp. But mine no less!" And the two faced each other. Truly:
    Ding Yuan stood for the right, and first lay dead;
    Yuan Shao now bares his blade, and risks his head.
Whether Yuan Shao kept his life, the next chapter will tell.

Plotting against Dong Zhuo, Cao Cao presents a blade

Now Dong Zhuo would have killed Yuan Shao, but Li Ru stopped him. "Things remain unsettled. There must be no rash killing." Yuan Shao bade the officials farewell and went out with the sword still in his hand, hung up his staff of office at the east gate, and rode away to Ji Province. Dong Zhuo said to Yuan Wei, the Grand Tutor: "Your nephew was insolent, but for your sake I pass it over. And what do you say to the change of emperors?" "You see it rightly, Grand Commandant," said Yuan Wei. "Whoever dares stand in the way of the great design answers for it under martial law," said Dong Zhuo. The ministers were cowed, and answered with one voice: "In everything we obey you." When the feast broke up, Dong Zhuo asked Zhou Bi the Palace Attendant and Wu Qiong the Colonel: "What will come of Yuan Shao's going?" Zhou Bi said: "He went off in a rage, and if you press the hunt for him he will make trouble for certain. Four generations of the Yuans have sown favors, and their pupils and their old subordinates are spread over the whole realm. If he gathers the bold spirits and draws a following, the heroes will rise at his back, and Shandong will be yours no longer. Better pardon him and make him Grand Administrator of some commandery. He will be glad to have his offense forgiven, and there will be no trouble." "Yuan Shao loves plans yet lacks decision," said Wu Qiong. "There is nothing to fear from him. Far better give him a commandery, and win the people's hearts by it."


Dong Zhuo took their advice, and that same day sent to appoint Yuan Shao Grand Administrator of Bohai. On the first of the ninth month he bade the Emperor ascend the Jiade Hall, and gathered the whole body of the officers, civil and military. Dong Zhuo drew his sword and held it in his hand. "The Son of Heaven is dull and weak, and not fit to be lord of the realm," he said to the company. "Here is the writ, which shall now be read out." And he had Li Ru read it: "Emperor Xiaoling early forsook his servants and his people. The Emperor succeeded to the line, and all within the seas looked up to him. But the Emperor is light and frivolous by nature, without reverence in his bearing, slack and idle in his mourning. His want of virtue is manifest, and he disgraces the great seat. The Empress Dowager's teaching has nothing of a mother's example in it, and her rule has been negligent and disordered. The Yongle Dowager died of a sudden, and men doubt the cause of it. Broken are the way of the Three Bonds and the ordering of Heaven and Earth. Xie, Prince of Chenliu, is great in sagely virtue, grave and exact in his conduct; he mourned with true grief, and no crooked word has passed his lips, and his good report and fair fame are known throughout the realm. He is fit to take up the great inheritance and carry the line down through ages to come. The Emperor is therefore put down to be Prince of Hongnong, and the Empress Dowager resigns the government. Let the Prince of Chenliu be raised to be Emperor, in answer to Heaven and in accord with men, to content the hope of all living."


When Li Ru had made an end of reading, Dong Zhuo barked at those about him to hand the Emperor down from the throne. They took the seal and its ribbon from him and made him kneel upright with his face to the north, owning himself a subject and waiting on command. The Empress Dowager too was called upon to put off her robes and await the decree. Emperor and Dowager wept aloud together, and every minister present grieved. Then one great officer below the steps cried out in fury: "Traitor Dong Zhuo, dare you lay a plot to cheat Heaven! The blood of my throat shall spatter you!" And he swung the ivory tablet in his hand straight at Dong Zhuo. Dong Zhuo in a great rage shouted to his guards to seize the man. It was Ding Guan of the Secretariat. Dong Zhuo had him dragged out and beheaded. Ding Guan cursed him without ceasing, his expression unchanged to the moment of death. A later poet sighed:
    The traitor Dong Zhuo nursed his dark design;
    To grass and rubble went the Han's great shrine.
    A court of ministers sat mute as stone.
    Ding Guan was man enough, and he alone.


Dong Zhuo then desired the Prince of Chenliu to go up into the hall. When the ministers had made their homage and their congratulation, Dong Zhuo had Empress Dowager He taken to the Yong'an Palace to live idle there, with the Prince of Hongnong and the Emperor's consort Lady Tang; the palace gates were sealed and barred, and no officer might enter without leave. Pity the young Emperor, who came to the throne in the fourth month and in the ninth was put down. The Prince of Chenliu whom Dong Zhuo set up was Xie, styled Bohe, second son of Emperor Ling — known to later ages as Emperor Xian — and he was nine years old. The reign style was changed to Chuping. Dong Zhuo became Chancellor of State. He was announced at court by his office and not his name, walked to his place without hastening his steps, and came up into the hall still armed and shod; for power to make or mar he had no equal. Li Ru urged him to raise men of name to office and so win the regard of the world, and spoke of Cai Yong's ability. Dong Zhuo sent to summon him, and Cai Yong would not come. In anger Dong Zhuo sent a man to tell him: "If you do not come, your whole clan will be destroyed." Cai Yong took fright and had no choice but to obey. Dong Zhuo was delighted to see him, advanced him three times in a month, made him a Palace Attendant, and held him dear.


Now the young Emperor was penned in the Yong'an Palace with Empress Dowager He and Lady Tang. Clothing and food grew scantier by degrees, and his tears were never dry. One day he chanced to see a pair of swallows flying in the court, and made a poem:
    The tender grass is green with gathered haze,
    A pair of swallows weave their slender ways.
    The Luo runs by, one thread of living blue;
    The men upon the road call blest the view.
    I gaze far off where azure cloud is rolled;
    There are the palace halls I had of old.
    Where is the man to take the loyal part,
    And vent the bitterness within my heart!


Dong Zhuo had men constantly on the watch, and that day they brought the poem to him. "He makes verses out of his grudge," said Dong Zhuo. "Now there is good ground to kill him." So he ordered Li Ru to take ten armed men into the palace and make an end of the Emperor. The Emperor was in an upper room with the Empress Dowager and Lady Tang when a palace maid announced Li Ru, and the Emperor was struck with terror. Li Ru offered him a cup of poisoned wine, and the Emperor asked what it meant. "The spring days are soft and warm, and Chancellor Dong sends up wine of long life," said Li Ru. "If it is wine of long life, you may drink first," said the Empress Dowager. "You will not drink?" said Li Ru angrily, and called his men to set a short blade and a length of white silk down before them. "If you will not have the wine, take one of these!" Lady Tang knelt and begged him: "Let me drink in the Emperor's stead, and spare the mother and the son their lives." Li Ru barked at her: "And who are you to die for a prince?" Then he lifted the cup to Empress Dowager He. "You shall drink first!" The Dowager fell to railing at He Jin and his want of judgment, that had brought the traitor into the capital and made this day's ruin. Li Ru pressed the Emperor hard. "Let me take my leave of the Empress Dowager," said the Emperor. He broke into bitter weeping and made a song:
    The earth is changed, the lights of heav'n decline;
    The throne is cast away, a princedom mine.
    A subject presses me, my days must wane;
    The great tide ebbs, and all my tears are vain!


Lady Tang made a song too:
    August Heav'n falls, and Sovereign Earth gives way;
    I am his lady, yet must live and stay.
    Living and dead on sundered roads we go;
    What shall I do? So early left in woe!


The song done, they clung to one another and wept. Li Ru barked at them: "The Chancellor of State waits for my report! Whose rescue do you expect?" The Dowager burst out cursing: "The traitor Dong Zhuo drives my son and me to this, and August Heaven does not protect us! You that serve his wickedness, your clans shall be destroyed to the last of them!" Li Ru in a great rage caught her up in both hands and flung her headlong down from the tower, shouted to his men to strangle Lady Tang, and poured the poisoned wine down the young Emperor's throat till he was dead. Then he went back and reported to Dong Zhuo, who had them buried outside the walls. From that time Dong Zhuo went into the palace every night, forced the palace women, and slept in the imperial dragon bed. Once he took his troops out of the city and came to the neighborhood of Yangcheng. It was the second month, and the village people were keeping the festival of the god of the soil, men and women gathered together. Dong Zhuo had his soldiers ring them round and kill the men, every one. He carried off the women and their goods loaded on carts, with above a thousand heads hung beneath, and came back to the capital in one long train, declaring that he returned from a great victory over brigands. Under the city gate he burned the heads, and parceled out the women and the goods among his soldiers.


Wu Fu, Colonel of Picked Cavalry, styled Deyu, had seen Dong Zhuo's cruelty and burned with indignation. He took to wearing a light coat of mail under his court robes with a short blade hidden in it, and watched for his chance. One day, as Dong Zhuo came to court, Wu Fu met him below the chamber, drew the blade and struck straight at him. Dong Zhuo was a strong man and caught him fast with both hands, and Lü Bu came in at once and hauled Wu Fu down. "Who taught you to rebel?" said Dong Zhuo. Wu Fu glared at him and shouted: "You are not my sovereign and I am not your subject. Rebellion against whom? Your crimes fill heaven, and every man alive would gladly have the killing of you! My one grief is that I cannot tear you apart with chariots and make amends to the realm!" Dong Zhuo in a great rage had him dragged out and cut to pieces, and to the moment of death the curses never left his lips. A later poet praised him:
    Of Han's last loyal men they tell Wu Fu,
    A heart that stormed the sky as none could do.
    He struck at the traitor in open hall;
    A man indeed, and so shall ages call.


From that day Dong Zhuo went nowhere without a guard of mailed men about him. Yuan Shao, at Bohai by this time, heard how Dong Zhuo played with power, and sent a man with a secret letter to Wang Yun. The substance of it ran: "The traitor Dong Zhuo cheats Heaven and has put down his sovereign, and his crimes are past speaking of; yet you let him work his will unchecked, as though you heard nothing of it. Is that the part of a servant who repays the state and keeps his loyalty? I am gathering troops and drilling men, meaning to sweep the royal house clean, but I dare not move rashly. If you are of the same mind, watch your chance and lay your plan. If you have any use for me, I am at your command."


Wang Yun had the letter, and turned it over in his mind, but could think of no plan. One day, in the waiting chamber at court, he saw the older ministers all together. "Today is this old man's birthday," he said. "Will you gentlemen stoop to come to my house this evening and take a cup?" "We shall certainly come and drink your health," they said. That evening Wang Yun set out a feast in the back hall, and the great officers all came. When the wine had gone round several times, Wang Yun suddenly covered his face and wept aloud. The company asked him in alarm: "Minister, it is your birthday. What is this grief?" "It is no birthday of mine," said Wang Yun. "I wished to speak with you all, and feared Dong Zhuo would suspect it. Dong Zhuo cheats his sovereign and plays with power, and the altars of the land may fall any morning or evening. Think how Gaozu cut down Qin and destroyed Chu and had the whole realm for his own. Who would have thought that, handed down to this day, it should be lost in Dong Zhuo's hands? It is for this that I weep." At that they all wept. Then one of the company clapped his hands and laughed aloud. "The whole court, weeping from dark to dawn and from dawn to dark again — can you weep Dong Zhuo to death?" Wang Yun looked, and it was Cao Cao, Colonel of Cavalry. "Your own forefathers ate the grain of Han," said Wang Yun angrily. "Have you no thought of repaying the state, that you sit there laughing?" "I laugh at nothing else but that among so many of you there is not one plan to kill Dong Zhuo," said Cao Cao. "Little as I am worth, I will strike Dong Zhuo's head off and hang it at the capital gate, and so make the realm amends." Wang Yun rose from his place. "What is your counsel, Mengde?" "These days past I have bent myself to serve Dong Zhuo for no other reason than to watch my chance at him," said Cao Cao. "He trusts me well enough now, and I can come near him. I hear you have the Seven Gems Sword, Minister. Lend it to me. I will go into the Chancellor's residence and kill him, and die for it with no regret!" "If Mengde is truly of that mind, it is great good fortune for the realm!" said Wang Yun, and poured him wine with his own hand. Cao Cao spilled a little on the ground and swore his oath, and Wang Yun fetched the blade and gave it to him. Cao Cao hid it about him, drank off his wine, and rose at once to take his leave of the company. The rest sat a while longer, and then they too broke up.


Next day Cao Cao girded on the blade and came to the Chancellor's residence, and asked where the Imperial Chancellor was. "In the small chamber," said the attendants. Cao Cao walked straight in. Dong Zhuo was sitting on the couch, with Lü Bu standing in attendance at his side. "Why so late, Mengde?" said Dong Zhuo. "My horse is a sorry one and goes slowly," said Cao Cao. Dong Zhuo turned to Lü Bu. "I have good horses sent in from Xiliang. Go yourself, Fengxian, and pick one out for Mengde." Lü Bu took the order and went. Cao Cao said within himself: "The traitor deserves to die!" He was on the point of drawing to stab him, but he feared Dong Zhuo's strength and dared not move rashly. Dong Zhuo, being fat and heavy, could not sit long, and lay back and turned his face to the wall. "Now he is done for!" thought Cao Cao, and whipped the blade out. He was about to strike when Dong Zhuo, looking up into the dressing mirror, saw Cao Cao behind him with the blade drawn, and turned sharply round. "What are you doing, Mengde?"


Lü Bu had that moment led the horse up outside the chamber. Cao Cao in his fright dropped to his knees with the blade held out. "I have a fine blade here, and would offer it to you, my gracious lord." Dong Zhuo took it and looked at it, and saw a blade above a chi long, inlaid with seven jewels and ground to a keen edge — a fine blade indeed. He handed it to Lü Bu to keep, and Cao Cao drew off the scabbard and gave him that too. Dong Zhuo brought Cao Cao out of the chamber to look at the horse, and Cao Cao thanked him. "Might I try a turn on him?" Dong Zhuo had him saddled and bridled. Cao Cao led the horse out of the Chancellor's residence, laid on the whip, and was away to the southeast. Lü Bu said to Dong Zhuo: "It looked to me just now as though Cao Cao meant to stab you, and when you called out and caught him he passed it off as a gift of the blade." "I thought the same," said Dong Zhuo.


They were still talking when Li Ru came in, and Dong Zhuo told him what had passed. "Cao Cao has no wife or children in the capital and lodges alone," said Li Ru. "Send to call him now. If he comes at once with no misgiving, it was a gift of the blade; if he makes excuses and does not come, it was an attempt on your life for certain, and he may be taken and questioned." Dong Zhuo thought well of it and sent four jailers to summon him. They were gone a good while, and came back with this: "Cao Cao never went back to his lodging. He rode out of the east gate at a gallop, and when the gate officer questioned him he said, 'The Imperial Chancellor sends me on urgent business,' then gave his horse its head and was gone." "He has a traitor's heart and has run for it," said Li Ru. "There is no doubt of the attempt now." Dong Zhuo raged: "I raised him so high, and he would have my life!" "He must have had accomplices," said Li Ru. "We shall know when Cao Cao is taken." So Dong Zhuo sent writs out everywhere for the arrest of Cao Cao, with a drawing of his face. Whoever took him and handed him over was to have a thousand in gold and a marquisate of ten thousand households, and whoever sheltered him was to share his guilt.


Now Cao Cao, once clear of the city, rode hard for Qiao Commandery. On the road he came through Zhongmou County, where the guard on the road took him and brought him before the Magistrate. "I am a traveling merchant, Huangfu by surname," said Cao Cao. The Magistrate looked hard at him and sat pondering a good while. "When I was at Luoyang seeking office I came to know you for Cao Cao. Why hide it? Put him in the lockup for the present; tomorrow we take him to the capital for the reward." He gave meat and drink to the men of the guard and sent them away.


In the middle of the night the Magistrate had his own men bring Cao Cao out secretly and take him into the back court to be questioned. "I hear the Imperial Chancellor treated you handsomely. Why bring this ruin on yourself?" "How should swallows and sparrows know the mind of the swan?" said Cao Cao. "You have caught me, so send me up and claim your reward." The Magistrate sent his people out of hearing. "Do not think so little of me. I am no common officer; it is only that I have not met my master yet." "My forefathers ate the grain of Han for generations," said Cao Cao. "If I had no thought of repaying the state, how should I differ from the beasts? I bent myself to serve Dong Zhuo only to watch my chance at him and rid the country of a plague. It has failed, and that is Heaven's will!" "And where were you bound, Mengde?" said the Magistrate. "I was for my own village, to put out a forged edict and call the lords of the realm to arms, that together we might destroy Dong Zhuo. That is my wish."


At that the Magistrate loosed his bonds with his own hands, helped him to the upper seat, and bowed twice. "You are indeed a loyal and upright man of the realm!" Cao Cao bowed in turn and asked his name. "Chen is my surname, Gong my name, Gongtai my style," said the Magistrate. "My old mother and my wife and children are all in Dong Commandery. Your loyalty has moved me, and I will give up my office and fly with you." Cao Cao was very glad. That night Chen Gong got money together for the road, and the two of them changed their clothes, slung a sword each on his back, and took horse for Cao Cao's own village.


They had been three days on the road, and came to the neighborhood of Chenggao as the light was failing. Cao Cao pointed with his whip into the deep of a wood. "There is a man here named Lü Boshe, my father's sworn brother. Let us go and ask him for news of my people, and a night's lodging. What do you say?" "Nothing better," said Chen Gong. They dismounted at the farm and went in to Lü Boshe, who said: "I hear the court has writs out everywhere and is hunting you hard, and your father has gone off to Chenliu out of the way of it. How do you come to be here?" Cao Cao told him all that had happened. "But for Magistrate Chen my bones would have been ground to powder by now." Lü Boshe bowed to Chen Gong. "But for Your Lordship, my young nephew's whole house would have been wiped out. Sit at your ease, Your Lordship; you shall have a bed in my poor cottage tonight." With that he rose and went within, and after a good while came out again. "I have no good wine in the house," he said to Chen Gong. "Let me go to the west village and buy a jar to set before you." And he got on his donkey in haste and rode away.


Cao Cao and Chen Gong had sat a good while when they heard the whetting of a knife behind the house. Cao Cao said: "Lü Boshe is no blood kin of mine, and there was something doubtful in his going. Let us listen." The two stole round behind the thatched hall, and heard a voice say: "Tie him up before we kill him. How will that do?" "There it is!" said Cao Cao. "Strike first, or we shall be taken." So the two drew their swords, went straight in and killed them all, men and women alike, eight of them one after another. Searching on to the kitchen, they found a pig bound there for killing. "You are too suspicious, Mengde," said Chen Gong. "We have killed honest people in error!" They hurried out of the farm, mounted and rode. Not two li on they met Lü Boshe coming with two jars of wine slung at his saddlebow and fruit and greens in his hand, and he called out to them: "Why are you off so soon, nephew, and Your Lordship?" "Men under a charge dare not stay long," said Cao Cao. "I told them at home to kill a pig for your dinner," said Lü Boshe. "Can you not spare one night, nephew, and Your Lordship? Turn your horses, I beg."


Cao Cao took no notice and whipped his horse on. He had gone only a few paces when he suddenly drew his sword, turned back, and called to Lü Boshe: "Who is that coming there?" Lü Boshe turned his head to look, and Cao Cao swung the sword and cut him down from the donkey. "Last was a mistake," said Chen Gong, appalled. "But what is this?" "Had Lü Boshe reached home and found so many dead, would he have let it rest?" said Cao Cao. "He would have raised the countryside and come after us, and that would have been our ruin." "To kill him knowing the truth is a great wrong!" said Chen Gong. "Better I wrong the world than the world wrong me," said Cao Cao. Chen Gong said nothing.


That night they rode on some li, knocked at the door of an inn by moonlight, and took lodging. When the horses were fed, Cao Cao lay down first and slept. Chen Gong lay thinking: "I took Cao Cao for a good man and gave up my office to follow him, yet he proves to be a cold-hearted wretch! Leave him alive today and he will be a plague hereafter." And he made to draw his sword and kill Cao Cao. Truly:
    No worthy man bears so malign a mind;
    Cao Cao and Dong Zhuo prove the same in kind.
Whether Cao Cao kept his life, the next chapter will tell.



Chapter 5
The lords rise at Cao Cao's forged edict
Three heroes break the pass and fight Lü Bu

Now Chen Gong was on the point of striking Cao Cao dead when a second thought stayed his hand. "I followed him here for the country's sake, and to kill him now would be a wrong. Better leave him and go elsewhere." He sheathed his sword, mounted, and rode for Dong Commandery without waiting for the light. Cao Cao woke, and Chen Gong was not there. "He thinks me a hard man for what I said, and has gone," he said to himself. "I must get on and stay here no more." So he rode through the night to Chenliu, found his father, and told him all that had passed; he would spend the family wealth, he said, to raise a righteous army. "Our means are too small, and I fear it will not do," said his father. "There is a Xiaolian here named Wei Hong, open-handed and staunch for the right, and his house is immensely rich. If he will help, the design can go forward."


Cao Cao set out wine and spread a feast, and asked Wei Hong to the house with all courtesy. He said: "The house of Han is without a master, and Dong Zhuo holds all the power, cheating his sovereign and harming the people, till the whole realm grinds its teeth at him. I would put out my strength to uphold the altars of the land, but the strength is not in me. You are a loyal and upright man, and I make bold to ask your help." Wei Hong said: "I have had that mind in me this long while, and only grieved that I had met no hero. Since Mengde has so great a purpose, my whole fortune is at your service." Cao Cao was overjoyed. He sent the forged edict out first, by fast riders to every quarter, and then set about raising his righteous army, putting up a white banner for the recruiting with the two words Loyalty and Righteousness upon it. Within a few days the men who answered came thronging thick as rain.


One day there came to join Cao Cao a man of Weiguo in Yangping, Yue Jin by name, Wenqian by style, and after him a man of Julu in Shanyang, Li Dian by name, Mancheng by style. Cao Cao kept them both as officers on his own staff. There came also a man of Qiao in the Principality of Pei, Xiahou Dun, styled Yuanrang, descended from Xiahou Ying. From a boy he had worked at spear and staff, and at fourteen he went to a master to learn arms; a man spoke foully of his master, and Xiahou Dun killed him and fled away out of the district. Hearing now that Cao Cao was raising troops, he came in with his younger cousin Xiahou Yuan, each of them bringing a thousand stout fellows. These two were Cao Cao's own cousins, for his father Cao Song was born a son of the Xiahou house and passed by adoption into the Cao family.


Within a few days two of his own clan, Cao Ren and Cao Hong, came to his help with above a thousand men apiece. Cao Ren's style was Zixiao and Cao Hong's Zilian, and both were practiced in the handling of troops and thoroughly expert at arms. Cao Cao was well pleased, and mustered and drilled his companies in the village. Wei Hong brought out the whole of his fortune for clothing and armor, flags and banners, and grain came in from every quarter past all counting.


By this time Yuan Shao had Cao Cao's forged edict, and he gathered his officers, civil and military, and came from Bohai with thirty thousand men to swear alliance with him. Cao Cao drew up a call to arms and sent it round the commanderies. It ran: "Cao Cao and his fellows make this proclamation to the realm in the great cause. Dong Zhuo cheats Heaven and defrauds Earth. He has brought a kingdom to ruin and murdered his sovereign, defiled the forbidden palace and butchered the living; wolfish and pitiless, he has heaped crime upon crime! We hold now a secret edict from the Son of Heaven, and gather a great righteous army, sworn to sweep the Middle Kingdom clean and cut down the whole brood of villains. Raise your righteous armies, and vent with us the wrath of all men. Uphold the royal house and save the common people. On the day this reaches you, act upon it with speed!"


When the call to arms had gone out, the lords of the garrisons rose in answer to it. The first was Yuan Shu, General of the Rear and Grand Administrator of Nanyang. The second, Han Fu, Inspector of Ji Province. The third, Kong Zhou, Inspector of Yu Province. The fourth, Liu Dai, Inspector of Yan Province. The fifth, Wang Kuang, Grand Administrator of Henei Commandery. The sixth, Zhang Miao, Grand Administrator of Chenliu. The seventh, Qiao Mao, Grand Administrator of Dong Commandery. The eighth, Yuan Yi, Grand Administrator of Shanyang. The ninth, Bao Xin, Chancellor of Jibei. The tenth, Kong Rong, Grand Administrator of Beihai. The eleventh, Zhang Chao, Grand Administrator of Guangling. The twelfth, Tao Qian, Inspector of Xu Province. The thirteenth, Ma Teng, Grand Administrator of Xiliang. The fourteenth, Gongsun Zan, Grand Administrator of Beiping. The fifteenth, Zhang Yang, Grand Administrator of Shangdang. The sixteenth, Sun Jian, Marquis of Wucheng and Grand Administrator of Changsha. The seventeenth, Yuan Shao, Marquis of Qi and Grand Administrator of Bohai. Their forces were of unequal strength, some thirty thousand and some ten or twenty thousand, and each came on toward Luoyang with his own officers.


Now Gongsun Zan, Grand Administrator of Beiping, was leading fifteen thousand picked men by way of Pingyuan County in Dezhou. As he went he saw far off, among a clump of mulberries, a yellow banner and a few horsemen riding out to meet him. He looked, and it was Liu Bei. "How do you come to be here, brother?" said Gongsun Zan. "It was by your recommendation, brother, that I was made Magistrate of Pingyuan," said Liu Bei. "Hearing that your army was passing this way I came out to wait on you. Come into the city and rest your horses." Gongsun Zan pointed at Guan Yu and Zhang Fei. "And who are these?" "Guan Yu and Zhang Fei, my sworn brothers," said Liu Bei. "The same who broke the Yellow Turbans with you?" "It was all their doing." "And what posts have they now?" "Guan Yu is a mounted archer, and Zhang Fei an archer on foot," said Liu Bei. Gongsun Zan sighed. "Here are heroes buried out of sight! Dong Zhuo is in revolt, and the lords of the realm are going up together to destroy him. Give up this petty office, brother, and come with us against the traitor to put out your strength for the house of Han. What do you say?" "I will go gladly," said Liu Bei. Zhang Fei said: "If you had let me kill the traitor that time, we should have been spared this day's work." "What is done is done," said Guan Yu. "Let us get ready and be off."


Liu Bei, Guan Yu and Zhang Fei rode with a few horsemen in Gongsun Zan's train, and Cao Cao met them. The rest of the lords came in one after another, and each pitched his camp, till the lines ran above two hundred li. Cao Cao killed oxen and horses for a great gathering of them all, and they debated how to advance. Wang Kuang, the Grand Administrator, said: "We are met in the great cause, and a chief of the alliance must be set up. Let all take his orders, and then march." Cao Cao said: "Four generations of Yuan Benchu's house have sat among the Three Ducal Ministers, and his gate is full of men who served under them of old. He comes of famous ministers of Han, and is fit to be chief of the alliance." Yuan Shao refused again and again. "None but Benchu will do," they all said, and then he consented. Next day a platform of three tiers was raised, with the banners of the five directions ranged about it and above them the white yak-tail standard and the yellow axe, the tallies of command and the general's seal. Then Yuan Shao was asked to go up. He set his robes straight and girded on his sword, climbed with a solemn air, burned incense, and bowed twice. The oath ran: "Misfortune is upon the house of Han, and the imperial order has lost its hold. The traitor Dong Zhuo has seized his opening to work ruin, bringing calamity on the Sovereign and loosing cruelty over the people. We, Yuan Shao and the rest, fearing that the altars of the land may be lost, have banded our righteous armies together and come as one to the country's need. We who are sworn in this bond shall be of one heart and strain together, to do a subject's duty and keep no second purpose. Whoever breaks this oath, let his life be struck from him and no seed of his remain. Let August Heaven and Sovereign Earth and the bright spirits of our forefathers be witness to it!"


The reading done, they smeared their lips with the blood of the sacrifice. The words had been so fervent that tears ran down every face. When the blood rite was over they came down from the platform, and the company helped Yuan Shao to the chief seat in the tent, the rest taking their places in two rows by rank and years. Cao Cao sent the wine round several times, and said: "Now that we have a chief of the alliance, let every man take his orders and uphold the state together, with no reckoning of strong and weak." Yuan Shao said: "I am a man of no great parts, but since you gentlemen have raised me to be chief of the alliance, merit shall have its reward and fault its punishment. The state has its standing penalties and an army its discipline. Let every man keep them, and none transgress." "In everything we obey you," they all said. Yuan Shao said: "My brother Yuan Shu shall have charge of the grain and forage, and see every camp supplied, so that none goes short. We need a man besides to lead the van, go straight down to Sishui Pass and offer battle. The rest will hold the strong ground and stand ready behind him." Sun Jian, Grand Administrator of Changsha, stepped out. "Let me lead the van." "Wentai is bold and fiery, and the charge is his," said Yuan Shao. So Sun Jian took his own men and horse and drove down upon Sishui Pass. The officers holding the pass sent a fast courier to the Chancellor's residence in Luoyang with word of their danger. Dong Zhuo, since all power had come into his hands, had feasted every day. Li Ru took the dispatch and brought it straight to him. Dong Zhuo was alarmed, and called his generals together in haste. Lü Bu, Marquis of Wen, stepped forward. "Rest easy, father. I look on the lords beyond the pass as chaff. Let me lead out an army of tigers and wolves, and I will have the heads off them all and hang them at the capital gate." "Having Fengxian, I may sleep easy!" said Dong Zhuo, delighted. Scarce had he spoken when a man behind Lü Bu called out: "Why use an ox-knife to kill a chicken? There is no need to trouble the Marquis to go himself. I will fetch you the heads of all those lords as easily as a man takes something out of a bag." Dong Zhuo turned and looked. The man stood nine chi, with a tiger's body and a wolf's loins, a leopard's head and an ape's arms, a man from west of the passes, Hua by surname and Xiong by name. Dong Zhuo heard him gladly, made him Colonel of Cavalry, gave him fifty thousand horse and foot, and sent him with Li Su, Hu Zhen and Zhao Cen to ride through the night to the pass and meet the enemy. Among the lords was Bao Xin, Chancellor of Jibei, who feared that Sun Jian, leading the van, would carry off the first honors. He secretly sent his brother Bao Zhong ahead with three thousand horse and foot by a byway, straight down to the foot of the pass to offer battle. Hua Xiong came flying down with five hundred mailed horsemen, shouting: "Stand, you rebel!" Bao Zhong turned in haste to fall back, but Hua Xiong's hand rose, the blade fell, and he was cut down from the saddle. Many of his officers were taken alive. Hua Xiong sent Bao Zhong's head to the Chancellor's residence with news of the victory, and Dong Zhuo made him a Commander.


Sun Jian came up before the pass with four generals of his own. The first was Cheng Pu, styled Demou, a man of Tuyin in Youbeiping, who carried an iron-spined serpent spear. The second, Huang Gai, styled Gongfu, a man of Lingling, with an iron mace. The third, Han Dang, styled Yigong, a man of Lingzhi in Liaoxi, with a great blade. The fourth, Zu Mao, styled Darong, a man of Fuchun in Wu Commandery, with a pair of blades. Sun Jian wore a coat of bright silver mail and a red headcloth, and sat a horse with a dappled mane, his Ancient Ingot Blade laid across the saddle. He pointed up at the pass and railed at them: "You knaves that take a traitor's part, why not yield at once!"


Hu Zhen, Hua Xiong's lieutenant, came out of the pass with five thousand men to give battle. Cheng Pu put his horse to the gallop, leveled his spear and made straight for him. They had not fought many bouts when the point took Hu Zhen in the throat and he fell dead from the saddle. Sun Jian waved his army on and drove right up to the pass, but arrows and stones came down from above like rain, and he drew his men back and camped at Liangdong, sending word of the victory to Yuan Shao and pressing Yuan Shu for grain. Someone said to Yuan Shu: "Sun Jian is the fierce tiger of Jiangdong. If he breaks into Luoyang and kills Dong Zhuo, we shall only have rid ourselves of a wolf to get a tiger. Send him no grain, and his army will break up of itself." Yuan Shu listened and sent out neither grain nor forage. Food ran short in Sun Jian's camp and his men fell into disorder, and spies carried word of it up to the pass. Li Su laid a plan before Hua Xiong: "Tonight let me take a force down by a byway and fall upon the back of Sun Jian's camp, while you drive at the front of it, General. Sun Jian will be taken."


Hua Xiong agreed, and gave the order for the men to eat their fill and go down from the pass under cover of night. The moon was bright and the wind clear. It was midnight when they came to Sun Jian's camp, and they drove straight in with drums and shouting. Sun Jian flung on his armor in haste, got to horse, and ran full upon Hua Xiong. The two closed, and they had not fought many bouts when Li Su's force came up behind and set the fires going. Sun Jian's men scattered in confusion. His generals were each left fighting where they stood, and only Zu Mao kept close to him as the two broke out through the ring and rode off. Hua Xiong came after them. Sun Jian took his arrows and loosed two, one after the other, and Hua Xiong dodged them both. Drawing for the third, Sun Jian pulled too hard and snapped his magpie-figured bow, and had to throw it away and ride for it. Zu Mao said: "The red headcloth on your head catches the eye, my lord, and the rebels know you by it. Take it off and let me wear it." So Sun Jian took it off and put on Zu Mao's helmet, and the two parted and rode different ways. Hua Xiong's men kept after the red headcloth only, and Sun Jian got clear by a byway. Zu Mao, hard pressed, hung the headcloth on a courtyard post of a house that the fire had not wholly consumed, and slipped into the wood to hide.


Hua Xiong's men saw the red headcloth from afar in the moonlight and ringed it round on every side, not daring to come near. They shot arrows at it and so found out the trick, then went up and took the headcloth. Then Zu Mao broke out from behind the trees, swinging his two blades to cut Hua Xiong down, but Hua Xiong gave a great shout, and with one stroke brought him out of the saddle. The killing went on till daybreak, and then Hua Xiong led his men back up to the pass. Cheng Pu, Huang Gai and Han Dang all found their way back to Sun Jian, who gathered his men together again and made his camp. He grieved without ceasing for the loss of Zu Mao, and sent a man through the night to carry word to Yuan Shao. "I never thought Sun Wentai would be beaten by Hua Xiong!" said Yuan Shao, dismayed, and he called the lords together to take counsel. They all came, Gongsun Zan last of them, and Yuan Shao had him in and seated with the rest. Yuan Shao said: "The other day General Bao's brother pushed forward on his own against orders, and threw his life away with a great many of our men. Now Sun Wentai too is beaten by Hua Xiong. Our men have lost heart. What is to be done?" None of the lords spoke.


Yuan Shao lifted his eyes and looked round the company, and saw three men of uncommon aspect standing behind Gongsun Zan, all of them smiling coldly. "Who are those behind you, Grand Administrator?" he asked. Gongsun Zan called Liu Bei out. "This is a brother of mine from boyhood, when we lodged together at our books: Liu Bei, Magistrate of Pingyuan." "Is this not the Liu Xuande who broke the Yellow Turbans?" said Cao Cao. "It is," said Gongsun Zan, and bade Liu Bei make his bow. Then he told them at length what Liu Bei had done and of what house he came. Yuan Shao said: "Since he is of the imperial line, bring a seat." And he bade him sit. Liu Bei would have declined it. Yuan Shao said: "It is not your name or your rank I honor, but that you are of the Emperor's own stock." So Liu Bei took the lowest seat, and Guan Yu and Zhang Fei stood behind him with folded hands.


A scout came in suddenly with word: "Hua Xiong has come down from the pass with his mailed horsemen, and carries Grand Administrator Sun's red headcloth on a long pole; he is before the camp, railing at us and offering battle." "Who will go out against him?" said Yuan Shao. Yu She, a bold officer, stepped out from behind Yuan Shu. "Let me go." Yuan Shao was pleased, and sent him out. Word came back at once that Yu She had not fought three bouts with Hua Xiong before Hua Xiong cut him down. They were all taken aback. Han Fu, the Grand Administrator, said: "I have a champion named Pan Feng, who can kill Hua Xiong." Yuan Shao sent him out at once, and Pan Feng took up his great axe and mounted. He had not been gone long when a rider came flying back with the news that Pan Feng too was cut down by Hua Xiong. The color went out of every face. "A pity my two chief generals Yan Liang and Wen Chou are not yet come!" said Yuan Shao. "With one of them here, what should I fear from Hua Xiong?" He had not done speaking when a man below the steps cried out: "Let me go and bring you Hua Xiong's head here before the tent!" They looked, and saw a man nine chi tall with a beard of two chi, the eyes of a red phoenix and brows like silkworms at rest, a face like a ripe date and a voice like a great bell, standing before the tent. Yuan Shao asked who he was. "That is Guan Yu, Liu Xuande's brother," said Gongsun Zan. Yuan Shao asked what post he held. "He follows Liu Xuande as a mounted archer." Yuan Shu roared at him from his seat: "Do you take us for lords with no great general? A mere archer dares talk so wildly! Throw him out!" Cao Cao stopped him quickly. "Be easy, Gonglu. The man would not talk so big without courage and skill behind it. Let him ride out and try, and if he does not win, there will be time enough to call him to account." Yuan Shao said: "Send out an archer to fight, and Hua Xiong will laugh at us for it." "The man's bearing is nothing common," said Cao Cao. "How should Hua Xiong know he is an archer?" Guan Yu said: "If I do not win, take my head."


Cao Cao had a cup of wine warmed and poured for Guan Yu to drink before he mounted. "Pour it out and let it stand," said Guan Yu. "I shall be back straightway." He went out of the tent, took up his blade, and sprang to the saddle. The lords heard the drums thunder outside the pass and a great shout go up, as though heaven were breaking and the earth falling in, the peaks shaking and the hills coming down, and fear took them all. Before they could send for news, the bridle bells rang out, the horse came in to the central camp, and Guan Yu threw Hua Xiong's head down on the ground. The wine was still warm. A later poet praised him:
    The first of feats, by heav'n and earth extolled;
    Before the camp the painted drums were rolled.
    Guan Yu set by his cup and proved his arm;
    He slew Hua Xiong, and still the wine was warm.


Cao Cao was greatly pleased. Zhang Fei came out from behind Liu Bei and shouted: "My brother has killed Hua Xiong! Why not cut our way through the pass this minute and take Dong Zhuo alive? What are we waiting for!" Yuan Shu flew into a rage. "We great ministers hold ourselves back, and a little trooper under a county magistrate dares swagger! Put the whole lot of them out of the tent!" "Reward the man who does the work," said Cao Cao. "What has high or low to do with it?" Yuan Shu said: "Since you gentlemen think so much of a county magistrate, I will take my leave." "Shall one word spoil the great business?" said Cao Cao, and he bade Gongsun Zan take Liu Bei, Guan Yu and Zhang Fei back to their camp for the time. The officers all dispersed, and Cao Cao sent a man secretly with beef and wine to comfort the three. Meanwhile the beaten remnant of Hua Xiong's men carried word up to the pass. Li Su wrote an urgent dispatch in haste and sent it in to Dong Zhuo, who called Li Ru, Lü Bu and the rest together to take counsel. Li Ru said: "We have lost our general Hua Xiong, and the rebels are strong. Yuan Shao is chief of their alliance, and his uncle Yuan Wei is Grand Tutor here. Should the two work together, we shall be in grave danger, and we had best remove Yuan Wei first. Lead the great army out yourself, Chancellor, and divide it to hunt them down."


Dong Zhuo thought well of it, and called Li Jue and Guo Si to take five hundred men and ring the house of Yuan Wei the Grand Tutor. They killed every soul in it, old and young alike, and Yuan Wei's head was sent ahead to the pass to be shown there. Then Dong Zhuo raised two hundred thousand men and came on by two roads. By the one he sent Li Jue and Guo Si ahead with fifty thousand to hold Sishui Pass and give no battle; he took a hundred and fifty thousand himself, with Li Ru, Lü Bu, Fan Chou and Zhang Ji, to hold Hulao Pass, which lies fifty li from Luoyang. When the army came up, Dong Zhuo ordered Lü Bu to pitch a great camp before the pass with thirty thousand men, while he lay upon the pass himself.


Scouts on fast horses carried word into Yuan Shao's great camp, and he called the company to counsel. Cao Cao said: "Dong Zhuo has his army at Hulao, and cuts our line across the middle. Half our force should be drawn off to meet him." So Yuan Shao detached eight of the lords — Wang Kuang, Qiao Mao, Bao Xin, Yuan Yi, Kong Rong, Zhang Yang, Tao Qian and Gongsun Zan — to go against him at Hulao Pass, while Cao Cao moved to and fro with his own men to support them. The eight set out, each with his own force, and Wang Kuang, Grand Administrator of Henei, came up first. Lü Bu came flying to meet him with three thousand mailed horsemen. Wang Kuang drew his men into line of battle and reined in under the standard to look, and Lü Bu rode out before his own line: on his head a three-pronged coronet of purple gold binding his hair, a battle robe of red Xichuan brocade figured with a hundred flowers, a coat of linked mail with beast faces at the shoulders, and a jeweled belt with lions worked upon it drawn tight over the armor; bow and arrows at his side, the crescent-bladed halberd in his hand, and under him Red Hare, that neighs into the wind. Truly: among men, Lü Bu; among horses, Red Hare!


Wang Kuang turned his head. "Who will go out against him?" A general behind him put his horse forward with his spear leveled, and Wang Kuang saw that it was Fang Yue, a noted man of Henei. The horses closed, and before five bouts Lü Bu's halberd had him out of the saddle; then Lü Bu leveled it and charged straight on. Wang Kuang's army broke and ran every way, and Lü Bu drove east and west among them as though he rode through empty air. By good luck the two forces of Qiao Mao and Yuan Yi came up to save Wang Kuang, and at that Lü Bu drew off. The three lords, each with some loss of men and horse, fell back thirty li and camped. The other five came up after them, and they took counsel together, agreeing that Lü Bu was a hero no man could match.


While they were still at their fears, a junior officer brought word: "Lü Bu is challenging us to battle." The eight lords mounted together and drew their men up in eight bodies on the high ground, and saw far off Lü Bu's company of horse, with embroidered banners fluttering, come on ahead to break their line. Mu Shun, an officer under Zhang Yang, Grand Administrator of Shangdang, rode out with his spear leveled to meet him; Lü Bu's hand rose, and one thrust of the halberd brought him down from his horse. A shock went through them all. Then Wu Anguo, an officer under Kong Rong, Grand Administrator of Beihai, came flying out with an iron hammer, and Lü Bu swung the halberd, clapped heels to his horse and came to meet him. They fought above ten bouts, and then one stroke cut through Wu Anguo's wrist; he dropped the hammer on the ground and ran. The men of the eight armies came out together and rescued him, and Lü Bu drew back. The lords returned to camp and took counsel. "There is no matching Lü Bu's valor," said Cao Cao. "Let the eighteen lords meet and find a good plan between them. Take Lü Bu, and Dong Zhuo will be easy to kill."


They were still debating when Lü Bu brought his men out again and offered battle, and the eight lords went out together. Gongsun Zan swung his lance and fought Lü Bu himself, but after a few bouts he broke and fled, and Lü Bu gave Red Hare its head and came after him. The horse went a thousand li a day and flew like the wind. Lü Bu was all but up with him, and raised the halberd to drive it between Gongsun Zan's shoulders, when a general — his great round eyes glaring, his tiger whiskers bristling — leveled the Serpent Spear and came flying up with a shout: "Stand, you slave of three surnames! Zhang Fei of Yan is here!"


Lü Bu saw him, left Gongsun Zan, and turned upon Zhang Fei. Zhang Fei gathered himself and closed with him fiercely. Above fifty bouts they fought together, and neither had the better. Then Guan Yu saw it, clapped heels to his horse, and came up swinging the Crescent Blade of eighty-two jin to take Lü Bu on the other side. The three horses closed in the shape of a T. Thirty bouts more, and Lü Bu was not to be beaten down. Then Liu Bei drew his pair of swords, put his yellow-maned horse to the gallop, and came in slantwise to help them.


The three ringed Lü Bu round and fought him, wheeling about him like the figures on a turning lantern, and the men of the eight armies stood staring. Lü Bu could hold his guard no longer; he feinted with the halberd at Liu Bei's face, and as Liu Bei dodged aside he broke open a corner of the ring, trailed the halberd behind him, and galloped off. The three would not let him go, but whipped their horses after him. The eight armies raised a great shout and swept forward together, and Lü Bu's people ran for the pass with Liu Bei, Guan Yu and Zhang Fei at their heels. Now Zhang Fei whipped his horse up to the foot of the pass, but arrows and stones came down from the wall like rain, and he could not force a way in. The eight lords had Liu Bei, Guan Yu and Zhang Fei in together to do them honor for the exploit, and sent a man to Yuan Shao's camp with word of the victory. Yuan Shao then sent written orders to Sun Jian to bring up his army. Sun Jian took Cheng Pu and Huang Gai with him to Yuan Shu's camp, and there, marking the ground with his staff, he said: "Dong Zhuo and I had no quarrel. Yet I have thrown myself forward regardless of my life and faced the arrows and the stones, to punish a traitor for the state, and to right the private wrong done to your own house. But you, General, give ear to slander and send out no grain, till I am beaten for the want of it. How could you!"


Yuan Shu, abashed, had nothing to say, and ordered the man who had carried the slander to be cut down, to make his peace with Sun Jian. Then word was brought in of a sudden: "A general from the pass has ridden into the camp and asks to see you." Sun Jian took his leave of Yuan Shu and went back to his own camp, and when the man was called in and questioned, it was Li Jue, a general whom Dong Zhuo favored. "What do you come for?" said Sun Jian. Li Jue said: "The Imperial Chancellor honors no man but you. He sends me now to make a match between the houses. He has a daughter, and would give her to your son." Sun Jian flew into a rage and shouted: "Dong Zhuo flouts Heaven and knows no law, and he has overturned the royal house. I mean to destroy his kindred root and branch, and so make amends to the realm. How should I marry with a traitor? I spare your head this once. Go back quickly and give up the pass, and you shall keep your life! Delay, and your bones will be ground to powder!"


Li Jue clapped his hands over his head and scuttled off like a rat, and came back to Dong Zhuo with the tale of Sun Jian's insolence. Dong Zhuo was furious, and asked Li Ru what should be done. Li Ru said: "The Marquis of Wen is lately beaten, and there is no fight left in the men. Better draw the army back to Luoyang and move the Emperor to Chang'an, and so answer the children's rhyme. They have been singing it in the streets these days past:
    At the western end, a Han;
    At the eastern end, a Han;
    Let the deer to Chang'an run,
    And the trouble shall be done.
As I read it, 'at the western end, a Han' answers to Gaozu, who flourished at the western capital Chang'an and handed the throne down through twelve emperors, while 'at the eastern end, a Han' answers to Guangwu, who prospered at the eastern capital Luoyang and has handed it down through twelve emperors now in his turn. The round of Heaven is come full and must turn back again. Move back to Chang'an, Chancellor, and there will be nothing to fear." Dong Zhuo was delighted. "But for your words I would never have seen it," he said. So he rode back through the night to Luoyang with Lü Bu, to take counsel on moving the capital. He gathered the officers civil and military in the council hall, and said: "Han has had its eastern capital at Luoyang two hundred years and more, and the breath of its fortune is on the ebb. The vigor of the age, as I see it, lies now at Chang'an, and I mean to attend His Majesty on his journey west. Get your baggage ready, all of you, and lose no time."


Yang Biao, Minister of the Masses, said: "The land within the passes is broken and desolate. To abandon the ancestral temple and forsake the imperial tombs with no cause will alarm the people. Nothing is easier than to stir the realm; nothing harder than to quiet it. I beg you to weigh it well, Chancellor." Dong Zhuo said angrily: "Would you thwart the great design of the state?" Huang Wan, the Grand Commandant, said: "Minister Yang speaks rightly. In the old days, when Wang Mang usurped the throne, and again in the time of the Gengshi Emperor and the Red Eyebrows, Chang'an was burned to a waste of tiles and rubble. The people were driven out of it, till not one or two in a hundred were left. To leave the palaces now for barren ground is not fitting." Dong Zhuo said: "The rebels are up east of the pass and the realm is tossed in disorder. Chang'an has Mount Xiao and Hangu Pass to guard it. It lies close to Longyou besides, so that timber, stone, brick and tile can be had at a day's notice, and the building of the palaces will not take above a month. Let me hear no more of this." Xun Shuang, Minister of the Masses, remonstrated: "If you will move the capital, Chancellor, the people will be in an uproar and have no rest." A great rage took Dong Zhuo. "I take counsel for the realm. What are the little people to me?" That same day he stripped Yang Biao, Huang Wan and Xun Shuang of their offices and made them commoners.


Dong Zhuo came out and got into his carriage, and saw two men bowing toward it. He looked, and they were Zhou Bi of the Secretariat and Wu Qiong, Colonel of the City Gates. Dong Zhuo asked what their business was. "We hear you mean to move the capital to Chang'an, Chancellor, and we came to speak against it," said Zhou Bi. Dong Zhuo rounded on them. "You two advised me to give Yuan Shao his office, and now he is in revolt. You are of one party with him!" And he shouted to his guards to thrust them out of the city gate and strike off their heads. Then he gave the order for the move, to begin the next day. Li Ru said: "Money and grain are short just now, and there are a great many rich houses in Luoyang whose goods may be listed and taken by the state. As for the clients of Yuan Shao and his fellows, kill their kin and party and seize all they have. The gain will be past counting."


Dong Zhuo sent out five thousand mailed horsemen to range the city and lay hands on the rich of Luoyang, some thousands of households in all. Flags were stuck above their heads, lettered large with the words Traitors and Rebels. They were driven outside the walls and killed to the last of them, their gold and goods seized. Li Jue and Guo Si drove the whole people of Luoyang, some millions of souls, out upon the road to Chang'an, setting a troop of soldiers between every company of the common folk, so that they dragged and pushed one another along. Those who died in the ditches were without number. The soldiers were let loose besides upon men's wives and daughters, and robbed the people of their food. The noise of the weeping shook heaven and earth. Whoever went too slowly had three thousand men at his back to urge him on, naked blades in their hands, killing as they went.


Before he went, Dong Zhuo had fire set at every gate, so that the dwellings of the people burned, and the ancestral temple and the palace offices with them. The flames of the North Palace and the South ran together, and the halls and courts of Luoyang lay all in ashes. He sent Lü Bu besides to break open the tombs of the late emperors, with those of the empresses and consorts, and take the gold and jewels out of them. The soldiers took the chance to dig up the graves of officers and commoners till scarcely one was left. Then Dong Zhuo loaded gold, pearls, silks and all that was good upon some thousands of carts, carried off the Son of Heaven, the empresses and the consorts, and set out for Chang'an.


Now Zhao Cen, one of Dong Zhuo's generals, found that Dong Zhuo had abandoned Luoyang and gone, and he gave up Sishui Pass. Sun Jian drove his men in first, while Liu Bei, Guan Yu and Zhang Fei cut their way into Hulao Pass, and the lords brought their armies in after them.


Sun Jian meanwhile rode hard for Luoyang, and saw from far off the flames going up to heaven and the black smoke lying along the ground. For two or three hundred li there was not a cock or a dog, nor the smoke of a hearth. He set his men first to putting out the fires, and bade the lords camp their companies on the waste ground. Cao Cao came to Yuan Shao and said: "Now that the traitor Dong Zhuo has gone west, this is the moment to press the advantage and hunt him down. Why do you keep your men still, Benchu?" Yuan Shao said: "The troops are all worn out, and I fear nothing will come of going on." Cao Cao said: "The traitor has burned the palaces and carried the Emperor away, and all within the seas is shaken and knows not where to turn. This is the hour Heaven has appointed for his ruin, and one battle will settle the realm. What do you doubt, that you will not advance?" The lords all said it would not do to move rashly. Cao Cao burst out: "These boys are not worth taking counsel with!" And he took above ten thousand men of his own, with Xiahou Dun, Xiahou Yuan, Cao Ren, Cao Hong, Li Dian and Yue Jin, and rode through the night after Dong Zhuo.


Dong Zhuo, for his part, had come to the neighborhood of Xingyang, where Xu Rong the Grand Administrator came out to meet him. Li Ru said: "You have only just left Luoyang, Chancellor, and we must look for pursuers. Let Xu Rong lay an ambush beside the fold of the hills outside Xingyang. If a force comes after us, let it pass clean by. When we have beaten them here, he can close the road behind and fall on them. Then those who come later will not dare follow." Dong Zhuo took the plan, and ordered Lü Bu to bring up picked men and hold the rear as well. Lü Bu was on the march when Cao Cao's army came up with him. "As Li Ru reckoned!" said Lü Bu with a laugh, and drew his men out in line. Cao Cao rode forward and shouted: "Traitor! You have carried off the Emperor and driven the people from their homes. Where would you go now?" Lü Bu railed back at him: "You turncoat and coward, how dare you talk so!" Xiahou Dun leveled his spear, put his horse forward, and made straight for Lü Bu. They had not fought many bouts when Li Jue came driving in with a force from the left, and Cao Cao sent Xiahou Yuan in haste to meet him. Then the shouting rose on the right as well, and Guo Si came up with another, and Cao Cao hurried Cao Ren out against him. There was no standing against three armies at once. Xiahou Dun could hold Lü Bu no longer and galloped back into his own line. Lü Bu brought his mailed horsemen down upon them, and Cao Cao's army was broken and ran back toward Xingyang. They came to the foot of a bare hill about the second watch, with the moon making it light as day, and only then did they gather up what was left of them.


They were just setting the pots to cook when shouting broke out on every side and Xu Rong's ambush came out upon them. Cao Cao whipped up his horse and forced a way out, but ran full upon Xu Rong and turned to fly. Xu Rong fitted an arrow and shot him in the shoulder. Cao Cao rode for his life with the arrow in him and turned past the slope of the hill, where two soldiers lay hidden in the grass. They saw his horse come on and drove in both spears at once, and the horse went down. Cao Cao was flung out of the saddle, and the two of them seized him. Then a general came galloping up, cut down both footmen with his blade, dismounted and raised him up. Cao Cao looked, and it was Cao Hong. Cao Cao said: "I am a dead man, brother. Save yourself!" "Quick, to horse!" said Cao Hong. "I will go afoot." "And what will you do when the rebels come up with you?" said Cao Cao. "The realm can do without me, but not without you." "If I survive this, I shall owe it to you," said Cao Cao, and got to horse. Cao Hong threw off his coat of mail, trailed his blade, and ran at the horse's side. They had gone on till past the fourth watch when a great river barred the road before them, with the shouting coming nearer behind. Cao Cao said: "My fate has brought me here, and I shall not get away alive!" Cao Hong pulled him down from the horse, stripped off his gown and armor, and carried him over on his back. They were barely on the far bank when the pursuers came up and shot at them across the water, and Cao Cao went on wet through. By daybreak they had made another thirty li and more, and rested a while under an earthen ridge. Then the shouting rose once more, and a body of horse and foot came after them. It proved to be Xu Rong, who had crossed the river higher up and followed them.


Cao Cao was in this strait when Xiahou Dun and Xiahou Yuan came flying up with a dozen horsemen, shouting: "Xu Rong, do our lord no harm!" Xu Rong made for Xiahou Dun, and Xiahou Dun leveled his spear and met him. A few bouts, and the spear went through Xu Rong and brought him down from the saddle, and the rest of his men scattered. Cao Ren, Li Dian and Yue Jin came searching afterward, each with his own troops, and found them. At the sight of Cao Cao, grief and gladness mingled in them. They gathered the remnant, above five hundred men, and went back in company to Henei. Dong Zhuo's army went on its way to Chang'an.


Now the lords camped separately in Luoyang. Sun Jian put out what fire was left in the palace and quartered his men within the city, setting his tent on the foundation of the Jianzhang Hall. He had his soldiers sweep the tiles and rubble out of the palace buildings, and every tomb that Dong Zhuo had broken open was covered and closed again. On the site of the Grand Ancestral Temple he ran up a rough hall of three bays, and asked the lords in to set the spirit tablets of the sainted emperors there, and an ox, a sheep and a pig were killed in sacrifice before them. When the rite was done they all dispersed, and Sun Jian went back to his camp. That night the stars and the moon shone together, and he sat out under them with his hand on his sword, looking up at the heavens. He saw a white vapor spread wide through the Ziwei enclosure, and sighed, and said: "The emperor star is dim, a traitorous subject throws the state into confusion, the people are trodden into the mire, and the capital stands empty!" Within that again lay a jade seal four cun square, the knob of it cut into five dragons twined together. He carried the stone away and brought it to King Wen of Chu, and when it was cut open there was the jade indeed. In the twenty-sixth year of Qin the jade workers were set to cut it into a seal, and Li Si wrote these eight characters upon it in seal script. In the twenty-eighth year the First Emperor was on progress as far as Lake Dongting when a great wind rose and the waves with it, and the boat was all but overturned. He flung the seal into the lake, and the storm stopped. In the thirty-sixth year he was on progress at Huayin, and a man barred the road with the seal in his hand and said to the Emperor's retinue: 'Carry this back to the Ancestral Dragon.' When he had said it he was gone, and so the seal came back to Qin. The year after, the First Emperor died. Ziying afterward gave it up to Gaozu of Han. Later, when Wang Mang usurped the throne, the Empress Dowager Xiaoyuan flung the seal at Wang Xun and Su Xian and broke a corner off it, and the corner was pieced out with gold. Guangwu came by this treasure at Yiyang, and it has passed with the throne down to this day. Of late, I hear, when the Ten Attendants threw the court into disorder and carried the young Emperor out to Mount Beimang, the seal was missing on the return to the palace. Gongsun Zan said to Liu Bei, Guan Yu and Zhang Fei: "Yuan Shao is good for nothing, and sooner or later there will be trouble. We had best go home." Liu Dai, Grand Administrator of Yan Province, asked Qiao Mao, Grand Administrator of Dong Commandery, for a loan of grain. Qiao Mao put him off and would not give it, so Liu Dai broke into his camp with his men, killed him, and took the surrender of all his following. Yuan Shao, seeing them scatter each his own way, led his troops out of camp as well, left Luoyang, and went away into the lands east of the pass.


Now Liu Biao, Inspector of Jing Province, styled Jingsheng, was a man of Gaoping in Shanyang and of the imperial house of Han. From a boy he loved to make friends, and he had seven men of name for his fellows, so that they were called in their day the Eight Worthies of Jiangxia: Chen Xiang of Runan, styled Zhonglin; Fan Pang of the same commandery, styled Mengbo; Kong Yu of the Principality of Lu, styled Shiyuan; Fan Kang of Bohai, styled Zhongzhen; Tan Fu of Shanyang, styled Wenyou; Zhang Jian of the same commandery, styled Yuanjie; and Cen Zhi of Nanyang, styled Gongxiao. To assist him he had Kuai Liang and Kuai Yue, men of Yanping, and Cai Mao, a man of Xiangyang. When he had read Yuan Shao's letter, he sent Kuai Yue and Cai Mao out with ten thousand men to cut Sun Jian off.

"You are a subject of Han," said Kuai Yue. "How come you to hide the treasure of the realm away for yourself? Leave it with us now, and you shall go free!" That angered Sun Jian, and he bade Huang Gai go out and fight. Cai Mao swung his blade and came to meet him. A few bouts, and Huang Gai brought his mace round upon Cai Mao and caught him full on the mirror plate over his heart. Now Sun Jian was surrounded by Liu Biao, and his three generals Cheng Pu, Huang Gai and Han Dang saved him at the hazard of their lives. He had lost more than half his men. He cut a road through and led the rest home to Jiangdong. From that day Sun Jian and Liu Biao were enemies.


Yuan Shao meanwhile lay with his army in Henei and was short of grain and fodder, and Han Fu, Governor of Ji Province, sent men with grain to keep his troops supplied. Feng Ji, one of Yuan Shao's counselors, said to him: "A great man ranges the realm at will. Is he to live on grain that another sends him? Ji Province is rich in money and grain. Why not take it, General?" Yuan Shao said: "I have no good plan for it." Feng Ji said: "Send a man secretly with a letter to Gongsun Zan, bidding him march on Ji Province, and promise to fall upon it from both sides at once. He will take the field for certain. Han Fu cannot plan for himself and will beg you to take the province in hand. Then do your work from within, and it is yours for the taking."


Yuan Shao was delighted and sent the letter off to Gongsun Zan at once. Gongsun Zan read that they were to attack Ji Province together and share the land between them, and in his joy he took the field that very day. Xun Chen said: "Gongsun Zan is driving the men of Yan and Dai straight down upon us, and there is no standing against their first rush. Han Fu said: "I served the Yuan house once, and I have not Benchu's abilities. The men of old sought out the worthier and gave place to him. Why should you gentlemen be jealous?" Geng Wu and Guan Chun sprang out with drawn blades to kill him, but Yan Liang, Yuan Shao's general, cut Geng Wu down where he stood, and Wen Chou hacked Guan Chun to death. Yuan Shao came into Ji Province, made Han Fu General of Might, and parted the business of the province among Tian Feng, Ju Shou, Xu You and Feng Ji, so that Han Fu was stripped of every power he had. Too late for regret, he left his household behind and rode away alone to Zhang Miao, Grand Administrator of Chenliu.

His followers fled back to Gongsun Zan with the news. Gongsun Zan was in a fury. "Yuan Shao drew me into taking arms against Han Fu, and then walked in and took the province himself! Now he has shot my brother down and laid it on Dong Zhuo's men. How should such a wrong go unavenged!" He called up every man of his own command and marched upon Ji Province.

Gongsun Zan halted his horse upon the bridge and shouted: "Faithless wretch, how dare you sell my name!" Yuan Shao rode up to the bridge in his turn and pointed at him. "Han Fu was not fit for his office, and yielded Ji Province to me of his own will. What business is that of yours?" Gongsun Zan said: "I took you once for a loyal and upright man and set you up as chief of the alliance. But now you show yourself a wolf at heart and a cur in all you do. What face have you to stand among men!" Yuan Shao cried in a great rage: "Who will take him for me?"


Before the words were out, Wen Chou put his horse forward with his spear leveled and came straight up onto the bridge. Gongsun Zan met him at the end of it. They had not fought a dozen bouts when Gongsun Zan could stand against him no longer and fled, and Wen Chou came after him hard. Gongsun Zan ran in among his own lines, and Wen Chou rode at a gallop into the middle, driving this way and that. Four generals of Gongsun Zan's came out against him together. Wen Chou ran one of them through and had him down from the saddle, and the other three fled. He chased Gongsun Zan out behind the lines, and Gongsun Zan made for a valley in the hills. Wen Chou whipped up his horse and shouted after him fiercely: "Down from your horse and yield!" Gongsun Zan had lost his bow and arrows, and his helmet had fallen from his head. His hair streaming loose, he drove his horse round the slope of a hill; the beast stumbled under him and pitched him down the bank. Wen Chou gripped his spear and came on to run him through, when out from the left of the grassy slope wheeled a young general, who came flying up with his spear leveled and made straight for Wen Chou.


Gongsun Zan scrambled up the bank and looked at the young man. He was eight chi tall, with heavy brows and great eyes, a broad face and a full jaw, and a commanding presence. He fought Wen Chou fifty or sixty bouts with neither getting the better of it. Then Gongsun Zan's men came up to the rescue, and Wen Chou wheeled his horse and went off. The young man did not follow him. Gongsun Zan hurried down the earthen bank and asked his name. The young man bowed from the saddle and answered: "I am a man of Zhending in Changshan. My surname is Zhao, my name Yun, my style Zilong. I was one of Yuan Shao's men, but seeing that he had no heart to serve his sovereign or to save the people, I left him to take service under you, and by chance I find you here." Next day Gongsun Zan drew his horse and foot into two bodies, left and right, spread out like a pair of wings. He had above five thousand horses, most of them white. In his old wars against the Qiang he had picked out white horses for the van, and was called the General of the White Horse for it. The Qiang would run at the mere sight of a white horse, so he had come to keep a great many. Yuan Shao set Yan Liang and Wen Chou in his own van with a thousand bowmen and crossbowmen apiece, and these likewise in two bodies, left and right, those on the left to shoot into Gongsun Zan's right wing, those on the right into his left. Then he put Qu Yi with eight hundred archers and fifteen thousand foot in the middle of the line, and held some tens of thousands of horse and foot behind them in support.


Gongsun Zan had but newly got Zhao Yun and did not yet know his heart, so he gave him a separate force and kept him in the rear, and sent his great general Yan Gang to lead the van. He took the center himself and halted his horse upon the bridge, with his standard planted beside him — a great red ring on it, and the word Commander worked in gold thread. They beat the drums from chen to si, but Yuan Shao's army did not come on. Qu Yi had his archers lie down under cover of their shields and wait for the signal shot before loosing. Yan Gang came on with drums and shouting and drove straight at Qu Yi, whose men lay still and never stirred at the sight of him, until he was close upon them. Then a shot went off, and the eight hundred bows and crossbows were loosed together. Yan Gang turned in haste to get back, but Qu Yi whipped his horse forward, swung his blade, and cut him from the saddle, and Gongsun Zan's army was broken. The two wings would have come to the rescue, but Yan Liang and Wen Chou held them off with their bowmen. Yuan Shao's whole line came forward and carried the fight to Jie Bridge, where Qu Yi rode in, cut down the standard-bearer first, and hewed the embroidered banner to the ground.


Gongsun Zan saw the banner go down, turned his horse, and rode off the bridge. Qu Yi drove his men straight on against the rear, and there came full upon Zhao Yun, who leveled his spear, put his horse forward, and made straight for him. A few bouts, and a single thrust brought Qu Yi down from his horse. Then Zhao Yun rode alone into Yuan Shao's army, breaking left and right as though not a man stood in his way. Gongsun Zan brought his men back to the attack, and Yuan Shao's army was routed.


Yuan Shao, for his part, had sent scouts forward, and they came back with word that Qu Yi had cut down a general, taken a banner, and was after the broken enemy. So he made no preparation, but rode out with Tian Feng to see it, taking a few hundred halberdiers of his own guard and a few dozen mounted bowmen, and laughing aloud as he went: "A worthless fellow, this Gongsun Zan!"


He was still speaking when Zhao Yun came charging up before him. The bowmen would have shot, but Zhao Yun ran several of them through one after another, and the rest all fled. Then Gongsun Zan's men ringed them round from behind. Tian Feng said to Yuan Shao in alarm: "Take cover within those empty walls, my lord!" Yuan Shao dashed his helmet to the ground and cried out: "A man would sooner die fighting in the line! Shall I creep behind a wall and hope to live?" At that the soldiers fought with one heart to the death, and Zhao Yun could not break through them. Then Yuan Shao's main body swept in, with Yan Liang bringing his force in as well, and the two fell on together. Zhao Yun covered Gongsun Zan and cut a way out through the press, and they came back to Jie Bridge. Yuan Shao drove his army forward and over the bridge after them, and there was no counting those who drowned. He led the chase himself, but he had not gone five li when a great shouting rose behind the hills, and a body of horse burst out with three generals at the head of it — Liu Bei, Guan Yu and Zhang Fei. Word had reached them at Pingyuan that Gongsun Zan and Yuan Shao were at war, and they had come to Gongsun Zan's aid. Now the three spurred forward together, each with his own weapon, and made straight for Yuan Shao. He was frightened out of his wits. The fine blade fell from his hand to the ground; he wheeled his horse and fled, and his men fought desperately to get him back across the bridge. Gongsun Zan drew off his men as well and went back to camp. When Liu Bei, Guan Yu and Zhang Fei had made their greetings, Gongsun Zan said: "If Xuande had not ridden so far to save me, I should have come off badly." And he had Zhao Yun brought to meet them, and Liu Bei took such a liking to him that he could not bear to part with him.


Yuan Shao, having lost a battle, held his ground and would not come out, and the two armies lay facing each other for more than a month, until word of it reached Dong Zhuo at Chang'an. Li Ru said to him: "Yuan Shao and Gongsun Zan are both men of mark in our day, and they are at each other's throats on the Pan River. Borrow the Emperor's name for an edict and send men to make peace between them. Both will be grateful for the favor and will come over to you, Grand Preceptor." Dong Zhuo liked the plan well, and next day he sent Ma Midi the Grand Tutor and Zhao Qi the Minister Coachman off with the edict. The two came into Hebei, and Yuan Shao went a hundred li out to meet them and bowed twice as he took it. Next day they went on to Gongsun Zan's camp and proclaimed the edict there. Gongsun Zan sent a man with a letter to Yuan Shao, and the two made peace. Ma Midi and Zhao Qi then returned to the capital with their report. Gongsun Zan marched home that same day, and sent up a memorial recommending Liu Bei for Chancellor of Pingyuan. Liu Bei and Zhao Yun parted holding each other's hands, with tears running, neither able to let the other go. Zhao Yun sighed and said: "I was wrong once in taking Gongsun Zan for a hero. Now I see what he does, and he is no better than Yuan Shao and the rest." Liu Bei said: "Bend yourself to his service a while. The day will come when we meet again." And they parted weeping.


Yuan Shu, meanwhile, was at Nanyang, and hearing that Yuan Shao had newly come by Ji Province he sent to ask him for a thousand horses. Yuan Shao would not give them. Yuan Shu was angry, and from that time the brothers were at odds. He sent also into Jing Province to borrow two hundred thousand measures of grain from Liu Biao, and Liu Biao would not give it either. Yuan Shu hated him for it, and sent a man secretly with a letter to Sun Jian, urging him to make war on Liu Biao. The letter ran, in substance: "It was my brother Benchu's doing that Liu Biao barred your road before this. Now Benchu is in private treaty with Liu Biao again, and they mean to fall upon Jiangdong. Raise your army quickly and make war on Liu Biao, and I will take Benchu for you, so that both wrongs are paid. You shall have Jing Province and I Ji Province. Do not fail in it!"


Sun Jian had the letter, and said: "Curse Liu Biao! He cut off my road home once, and if I do not take the moment now and pay him for it, when shall I?" He called Cheng Pu, Huang Gai, Han Dang and the rest into his tent to consider it. Cheng Pu said: "Yuan Shu is full of guile and not to be trusted." Sun Jian said: "I mean to have my own revenge. Why would I need Yuan Shu's help?" So he sent Huang Gai down to the river first to get the warships ready, with arms and grain loaded aboard them and the horses in the great ships, and set a day to march. Spies on the river learned of it and brought word to Liu Biao, who was much startled and called his officers, civil and military, together in haste. Kuai Liang said: "There is no need to be troubled. Let Huang Zu lead the men of Jiangxia out in the van, while you follow with the men of Jing and Xiang in support, my lord. Coming across rivers and over lakes, how can Sun Jian bring his strength to bear?" Liu Biao thought well of it. He bade Huang Zu make ready, and brought up his great army behind.


Now Sun Jian had four sons by Lady Wu. The eldest was Sun Ce, styled Bofu; the second Sun Quan, styled Zhongmou; the third Sun Yi, styled Shubi; the fourth Sun Kuang, styled Jizuo. Lady Wu's younger sister was Sun Jian's second wife, and she bore him a son and a daughter besides: the son Sun Lang, styled Zaoan, and the daughter Sun Ren. Sun Jian had also taken a son of the Yu family into his own house, Sun Shao, styled Gongli. And he had a younger brother, Sun Jing, styled Youtai.


As Sun Jian was setting out, Sun Jing brought the sons to kneel in a row before his horse, and spoke against it: "Dong Zhuo has all power in his hands, the Son of Heaven is weak, and all within the seas is in uproar, each lord the master of his own quarter. Jiangdong has but now come to a little peace, and to raise a great army over one small grudge is ill done. I beg you to reconsider." Sun Jian said: "Enough of this, brother. I mean to range the realm at will. Am I to leave an injury unpaid?" Then his eldest son Sun Ce said: "If you must go, father, let me go with you." Sun Jian allowed it, and the two took ship together and drove for Fancheng.


Huang Zu had bowmen and crossbowmen lying along the bank, and when the ships came in to shore they let fly a storm of arrows. Sun Jian ordered his men not to stir, but to lie close in the ships and draw the enemy on by sailing in and out. For three days the ships came in to shore some scores of times, and Huang Zu's men did nothing but shoot, until their arrows were all spent. Then Sun Jian pulled out the arrows his ships had caught, something over a hundred thousand of them. The wind set fair that day, and he had his soldiers loose, until those on the bank could not hold their ground and fell back.


Sun Jian's men came ashore. Cheng Pu and Huang Gai parted their force in two and bore straight down on Huang Zu's camp, while Han Dang drove his men hard up behind. Pressed on three sides, Huang Zu was badly beaten. He gave up Fancheng and fell back into Dengcheng. Sun Jian left Huang Gai to keep the ships and led the pursuit himself. Huang Zu brought two generals out with him, Zhang Hu of Jiangxia and Chen Sheng of Xiangyang. Huang Zu raised his whip and railed: "Rats and thieves of Jiangdong! How dare you break into the lands of an imperial kinsman!" And he sent Zhang Hu out to challenge. Han Dang came out of Sun Jian's line to meet him. The two horses closed, and they had fought thirty bouts and more when Chen Sheng, seeing Zhang Hu's strength fail, came flying up to help him. Zhang Hu saw Chen Sheng fall, and the shock of it caught him off guard. Han Dang's blade took half his head off. Sun Jian cut down the fugitives as far as the Han River, and bade Huang Gai bring the ships up to lie there.


Huang Zu gathered up what was left of his men and came to Liu Biao, telling him there was no standing against Sun Jian's force. Liu Biao in his alarm called in Kuai Liang to advise him. Kuai Liang said: "We are freshly beaten and the men have no heart for a fight. We can only keep behind deep ditches and high walls, and let their first attack go by, while we send secretly to Yuan Shao for help. Then the siege will lift of itself." Cai Mao said: "Zirou's plan is a poor one. The enemy is at the foot of our wall and his generals are on the river. Are we to fold our hands and wait to be killed? I am of no great parts, but give me men to lead out of the city, and I will settle it in one battle." Liu Biao gave him leave.


Cai Mao led above ten thousand men out of Xiangyang and drew up on Mount Xian. Sun Jian came driving on with his victorious army. Cai Mao rode out. Sun Jian said: "That is the brother of Liu Biao's second wife. Who will take him for me?" Cheng Pu leveled his iron-spined spear and went out against him. Within a few bouts Cai Mao broke and ran. Sun Jian pushed his whole army forward, and the dead lay strewn across the field. Cai Mao fled into Xiangyang. Kuai Liang said that Cai Mao had refused good counsel and brought on this great defeat, and that by the law of the army he should lose his head. But Liu Biao had lately married his sister, and did not punish him.


Sun Jian set his force on all four sides of Xiangyang, shut the city in, and attacked it. Then one day a wild wind sprang up and snapped the staff of the commander's banner in the center camp. Han Dang said: "This is no lucky sign. We had best draw off for the present." Sun Jian said: "I have fought and won over and over, and Xiangyang will be mine within a day or two. Am I to break off the war because the wind has broken a flagstaff?" So he would not listen to Han Dang, and pressed the attack harder than before. Kuai Liang said to Liu Biao: "I watched the sky last night and saw a general's star about to fall. Reckoning by field allotment, it must answer to Sun Jian. Send a letter to Yuan Shao quickly, my lord, and ask his help."


Liu Biao wrote the letter and asked who would dare break out through the siege with it. A general named Lü Gong answered at once that he would go. Kuai Liang said: "Since you dare go, hear my plan. Take five hundred horse and foot, with as many good bowmen among them as you can, and break out through their lines. Then make for Mount Xian. They will send men after you for certain. Put a hundred of yours up the hill to gather stones and have them ready, and a hundred with bows and crossbows in ambush in the wood. When the pursuit comes up, do not run straight on, but wind and double and draw them to the place of the ambush; then let stones and arrows go together. If you succeed, send up a string of signal shots, and the city will come out to your aid. If there is no pursuit, give no signal, but ride on your way. The moon is dim tonight, and you may go out of the city at dusk."


Lü Gong took his orders and got his men ready. At dusk they opened the east gate quietly, and he led them out of the city. Sun Jian was in his tent when he heard shouting, and he got to horse in haste with thirty-odd riders and came out of camp to see. A soldier reported that a body of cavalry had cut its way out and gone off toward Mount Xian. Sun Jian did not wait to call his generals, but rode after them with the riders. Lü Gong had already laid his men above and below among the thick trees of the hill. Sun Jian's horse was fast, so he outstripped the rest and came on alone, with the men he chased not far ahead of him. "Stand!" he shouted. Lü Gong reined round and came back to fight him. They closed, and at a single bout Lü Gong broke away and slipped into a path among the hills. Sun Jian went in after but lost sight of him. He was about to go up the hill when a gong sounded, and the stones came pelting down from above while the arrows flew out of the wood together. Sun Jian took the stones and the arrows in his body, his brains ran out, and horse and man died there on Mount Xian. He was but thirty-seven years old.


Lü Gong cut off the thirty riders and killed every one of them, then sent up the string of signal shots. Huang Zu, Kuai Yue and Cai Mao led their men out of the city by separate ways, and the army of Jiangdong fell into confusion. Huang Gai heard the shouting shake the sky and brought his naval force up to the fight, where he met Huang Zu face to face. In less than two bouts he captured him alive. Cheng Pu, guarding Sun Ce, was hunting for a way out when he came upon Lü Gong. He drove forward, and within a few bouts his spear had Lü Gong out of the saddle. The two armies fought on till daybreak, then each drew off, and Liu Biao's men went into the city. When Sun Ce came back to the Han River he learned that his father had been shot dead, and that Liu Biao's soldiers had carried the body into the city. He wept aloud, and the whole army wept with him. "My father's body is there," he said. "How can I return home?" Huang Gai said: "We have Huang Zu alive here. Let a man go into the city and treat for peace, and we will give Huang Zu for my lord's body."


Before he had done speaking, an officer of the army named Huan Kai came forward. "I am an old acquaintance of Liu Biao's. Let me go into the city as envoy." Sun Ce allowed it, and Huan Kai went in to Liu Biao and laid the whole proposal before him. Liu Biao said: "I have Wentai's body here, and have laid it in a coffin. Send Huang Zu back quickly, and let both sides lay down their arms and make no more inroads on each other." Huan Kai bowed his thanks and was about to go when Kuai Liang came forward from the foot of the hall. "It must not be! I have a word to say, and not a man of the Jiangdong army shall get home alive. Let Huan Kai be beheaded first, and then hear my plan." Truly:

Now Kuai Liang said: "Sun Jian is dead and his sons are all young. Strike while they are weak. March at once, and Jiangdong may be had at a single roll of the drums. Give back the body and break off the war, and you leave them time to grow strong, which will be the undoing of Jing Province." Liu Biao said: "Huang Zu is in their camp. How can I bear to leave him in their hands?" Kuai Liang said: "Why not give up one witless Huang Zu and take Jiangdong?" Liu Biao said: "Huang Zu and I are bosom friends. To cast him off would not be right." So he sent Huan Kai back to the camp, and it was agreed between them that Sun Jian's body should be exchanged for Huang Zu.


Liu Biao had Huang Zu back in exchange, and Sun Ce received his father's coffin, drew off his army and went home to Jiangdong, where he buried him on the plain of Qu'e. When the mourning was done he led his army to Jiangdu and settled there, calling the worthy to him, taking able men into his service, and bending his own pride to all who came, so that little by little the bold spirits of the four quarters gathered to him. But of that, no more here.


Now Dong Zhuo was at Chang'an, and hearing that Sun Jian was dead he said: "The thorn is plucked that in my bosom bred!" He asked how old the son was. Someone answered that he was seventeen, and Dong Zhuo thought no more of it. From that time he grew haughtier than ever. He styled himself Esteemed Father, and when he went out he usurped the pomp and escort of the Son of Heaven. His younger brother Dong Min he made General of the Left and Marquis of Hu, and his nephew Dong Huang Palace Attendant with the whole command of the palace guards. Every man of the house of Dong, old or young, was made a full marquis. Two hundred and fifty li from Chang'an he raised a stronghold of his own, Meiwu, and set two hundred and fifty thousand of the common people to build it. Its walls stood as high and ran as thick as the walls of Chang'an, and within were halls and storehouses with grain laid up for twenty years. He chose eight hundred young beauties out of the people to fill it. Gold and jade, colored silks and pearls were heaped there beyond all reckoning. His household lived within, and he came and went between Meiwu and Chang'an, now once in half a month, now once in a month, with the great officers waiting outside the Heng Gate to see him away.


Dong Zhuo would often have a tent set up beside the road and drink there with the great officers. One day, as he went out by the Heng Gate with the whole court to see him away, he kept them to a feast, and just then some hundreds of soldiers from Beidi, who had laid down their arms on a promise of pardon, were brought up. Dong Zhuo had them dealt with in front of the company. The hands and feet of some were struck off, the eyes of some gouged out, the tongues of some cut away, and some were boiled in a great cauldron. The howling of them shook the sky, and the officials trembled till the chopsticks fell from their hands, while Dong Zhuo ate and drank, talked and laughed, quite at his ease.


Another day Dong Zhuo called the whole body of officials together at the offices of state, and they sat in two long rows. The wine had gone round several times when Lü Bu walked straight in and said a few words in Dong Zhuo's ear. Dong Zhuo laughed. "So that is how it stands," he said, and bade Lü Bu drag Zhang Wen, the Minister of Works, from his place at the feast and down out of the hall. The officials paled. In a little while an attendant came in and presented Zhang Wen's head upon a red tray, and every man there sat half dead with fright. Dong Zhuo said, smiling: "Be easy, gentlemen. Zhang Wen was in league with Yuan Shu and plotted to do me harm. A man carrying a letter between them delivered it by mistake at my son Fengxian's house, and so I had Zhang Wen beheaded. You have done nothing, and need not be afraid." The officials murmured their assent and broke up.


Wang Yun, Minister of the Masses, went back to his house turning over in his mind what had passed at that feast, and could not rest in his seat. Late at night, with the moon up, he took his staff and walked out into the back garden, where he stood beside the rose trellis and looked up to heaven, and the tears fell. Then he heard someone by the peony pavilion, heaving sigh after sigh. He crept up to look, and it was Diaochan, a singing girl of his own household. She had been chosen into the house as a child and taught to sing and dance. Now just sixteen, she was as skilled as she was fair, and Wang Yun treated her as his own daughter. He had listened a good while before he called out: "Wretch! Is this some love affair of yours?" Diaochan started and dropped to her knees. "How should I dare have any such thing!" Wang Yun said: "If there is none, what are you sighing for so late at night?" "Let me tell you what is in my heart." "Hide nothing. Tell me the truth of it." Diaochan said: "You have fed and reared me, sir, had me taught to sing and dance, and treated me better than I deserve. If my body were ground to dust I could not repay the least part of it. Of late I have seen your brows knotted with care, and knew there must be some great matter of state upon you, but I dared not ask. I sighed because tonight I saw again that you could neither sit nor stand at ease. I never thought you would be watching me. If there is any use you can put me to, I would not refuse it though it cost me my life." Wang Yun struck the ground with his staff. "Who would have thought the realm of Han should lie in your hands! Come with me to the painted chamber."


Diaochan followed Wang Yun into the chamber. He drove out every maid and concubine, seated her, and knocked his head on the floor before her. She threw herself down in alarm. "Why do you do this, sir?" "Pity the living souls of Han!" said Wang Yun, and the tears came from him like water from a spring. "I said just now that I would not refuse any command of yours, though it cost me my life." Then Wang Yun knelt to her. "The people hang head downward, and ruler and ministers are no safer than eggs piled in a heap. There is none but you who can save them. The traitor Dong Zhuo means to usurp the throne, and not a man at court has a plan against him. Dong Zhuo has a foster son, Lü Bu by name, of uncommon courage and strength, and both of them, as I read them, love women too well. So I would lay a double snare for them. First I shall promise you in marriage to Lü Bu, and then present you to Dong Zhuo. Between the two of them you must watch your chance, and contrive to set father and son at odds, until Lü Bu kills Dong Zhuo and the great evil is finished. If the altars of the land are propped up again and the state set on its feet, it will all be your doing. How does it seem to you?" Diaochan said: "I have told you I would not refuse death. Give me to them, and I shall know how to manage it." "If this leaks out, my whole house will be destroyed." "Have no fear of it, sir. If I fail this great cause, let me die under a thousand blades."


Wang Yun bowed his thanks to her. Next day he took some bright pearls out of the store of his house, had good craftsmen set them in a coronet of gold, and sent it secretly to Lü Bu. Lü Bu was overjoyed, and came himself to Wang Yun's house to thank him. Wang Yun had a fine dinner made ready, and when Lü Bu came he went out to the gate to receive him, brought him into the back hall, and set him in the seat of honor. Lü Bu said: "I am no more than a general of the Chancellor's residence, and you, Minister, are a great officer of the court. Why show me courtesy so far beyond my place?" Wang Yun said: "There is no hero in the realm today but you, General. It is not your office I honor, but your gifts." Lü Bu was well content. Wang Yun pressed the wine on him with every courtesy, and never left off praising Dong Zhuo's goodness and Lü Bu's own, till Lü Bu laughed aloud and drank his fill. Then Wang Yun sent the servants away and kept only a few women to pour the wine, and when it had warmed them both he said: "Call the child in."


Presently two maids in green led Diaochan out in all her finery. Lü Bu asked in surprise who she was. "My daughter Diaochan," said Wang Yun. "You have been so good to me, General, beyond anything I deserve, that I count you no stranger but one of my own blood, and so I have let her come out to see you." And he bade Diaochan fill Lü Bu's cup. She carried the wine to him, and glances passed between them. Wang Yun, feigning drunkenness, said: "The child begs you to drink deep, General. My whole house leans upon you." Lü Bu asked her to sit, but she made as if to withdraw. "The General is my dearest friend," said Wang Yun. "Where is the harm in sitting down?" So Diaochan sat down at Wang Yun's side, and Lü Bu never took his eyes off her.


After a few cups more Wang Yun pointed to Diaochan and said: "I would give this girl to you, General, for a concubine. Will you have her?" Lü Bu rose from his place and thanked him. "If that may be, I am yours to command." Wang Yun said: "One of these days I will choose a lucky hour and send her to your house." Lü Bu's joy was past all bounds, and his eyes kept turning to Diaochan, and she answered them with her own. The company broke up soon after. Wang Yun said: "I would have kept you the night, General, but the Grand Preceptor might grow suspicious." Lü Bu thanked him over and over and went away.


Some days later, in the council hall, Wang Yun saw Dong Zhuo, and noticing Lü Bu was not at his side, he bowed to the ground and made his request: "I beg you to turn your carriage aside to my humble house and dine there, Grand Preceptor, if it should please you." Dong Zhuo said: "If you invite me, Minister, I will come at once." Wang Yun gave his thanks and went home, where he set out every dainty of land and water, placed the seat in the middle of the front hall, laid brocade on the floor and hung curtains within and without. At noon the next day Dong Zhuo came. Wang Yun in his court robes went out to meet him, bowed twice and gave him greeting. Dong Zhuo got down from his carriage, and above a hundred mailed men with halberds crowded in with him to the hall and ranged themselves on either side. Wang Yun made his two bows at the foot of the hall, and Dong Zhuo bade them raise him and gave him a seat next his own. Wang Yun said: "Your virtue towers so high that not Yi Yin nor the Duke of Zhou could come near it." Dong Zhuo was mightily pleased. The wine went round and the music played, and Wang Yun paid him every honor.


When it grew late and they were flushed with wine, Wang Yun asked Dong Zhuo into the back hall, and Dong Zhuo sent his guards away. Wang Yun raised his cup in congratulation. "I have studied the stars a little since I was a boy, and watching the signs of heaven at night I find that the fated years of the house of Han are run out. Your merit and virtue shake the realm, Grand Preceptor, and were you to take the throne as Shun took it from Yao, and Yu from Shun, it would be what Heaven wills and men desire." Dong Zhuo said: "How should I dare look so high!" Wang Yun said: "It has been said from of old that he who has the Way strikes down him who has not, and he who lacks virtue gives place to him who has it. How should that be presumptuous?" Dong Zhuo said with a laugh: "If the mandate of Heaven does indeed come to me, you shall stand first in merit, Minister."


Wang Yun thanked him. Painted candles were lit in the hall, and none were left there but the serving women to bring the wine and the dishes. Wang Yun said: "The Imperial Music Office has nothing good enough to set before you. I have a singer of my own household, and make bold to bid her wait upon you." Dong Zhuo said: "Very good." So Wang Yun had the blinds let down, the reed pipes wound their music round the hall, and Diaochan was led out to dance beyond the blind. There is a lyric in praise of her:
    Born to the Zhaoyang halls in days of old,
    A startled swan, a form one palm could hold.
    Across Lake Dongting, borne upon the breeze,
    She treads the Liangzhou measure through with ease,
    A fresh new blossom that the breezes swing;
    The painted hall's warm scent must yield to spring.
And a poem too:
    Quick flies the swallow to the clappers' call,
    A drift of cloud comes to the painted hall.
    Her dark brows the wanderer's grief impart,
    Her face at first sight breaks the old love's heart.
    No gold could ever buy a smile so rare;
    The willow's sash requires no gem to wear.
    Through the drawn blind they steal a parting view;
    And none can tell which man is king of Chu.


The dance done, Dong Zhuo bade her come near, and Diaochan stepped inside the blind and bowed low twice. Dong Zhuo saw her beauty, and asked: "Who is this girl?" Wang Yun said: "Diaochan, a singing girl of my house." "Can she sing for us?" Then Wang Yun bade Diaochan take the wooden clappers and sing a song. Truly:
    One cherry mouth unclosing, red lips sing,
    Two rows of jade breathe out the sunny spring.
    A tongue of clove spits out a steely blade,
    To cut the traitor down who wrecks the state.


Dong Zhuo could not praise her enough, and Wang Yun bade Diaochan fill his cup. Dong Zhuo raised it. "How old are you?" "I am just sixteen," said Diaochan. Dong Zhuo laughed. "Truly one of the immortals!" Wang Yun rose. "I would present this girl to you, Grand Preceptor, if you will take her." Dong Zhuo said: "How am I to repay such a favor?" Wang Yun said: "To wait upon you is more than she deserves." Dong Zhuo thanked him many times over. Wang Yun ordered a felt carriage made ready at once and sent Diaochan on ahead to the Chancellor's residence. Dong Zhuo rose and took his leave, and Wang Yun went with him the whole way to the residence before turning back. He had not ridden half the road home when he saw two rows of red lanterns lighting the way, and Lü Bu came riding up with his halberd in his hand. They met face to face. Lü Bu reined in, caught Wang Yun by the breast of his robe, and demanded fiercely: "You promised Diaochan to me, Minister, and now you have sent her to the Grand Preceptor. Do you make a fool of me?" Wang Yun cut him short. "This is no place to talk. Come to my humble house."


Lü Bu went with Wang Yun to his house, got down from his horse and came into the back hall. When the greetings were over Wang Yun said: "Why are you angry with this old man, General?" Lü Bu said: "I am told you sent Diaochan to the Chancellor's residence in a felt carriage. What is the meaning of it?" Wang Yun said: "Then you have not heard it all, General! Yesterday in the council hall the Grand Preceptor said to me: 'I have a matter in hand, and I will come to your house.' So I made ready and waited for him. Over the wine he said: 'I hear you have a daughter called Diaochan, and that you have promised her to my son Fengxian. I was afraid your word might not hold, and I have come on purpose to ask for her and see her.' I dared not cross him, and brought Diaochan out to make her bow to her father-in-law. Then the Grand Preceptor said: 'Today is a lucky day. I will take the girl home with me now and give her to Fengxian.' General, with the Grand Preceptor himself come to my house, how should this old man dare refuse him?" Lü Bu said: "Forgive me, Minister. I was mistaken for a moment, and tomorrow I will come and beg your pardon." Wang Yun said: "The girl has a little dowry, and when she has gone over to your house, General, I will send it after her."


Lü Bu thanked him and went away. Next day he made inquiries at the Chancellor's residence and heard not a word, so he pushed on into the hall and questioned the waiting-women. One of them answered: "The Grand Preceptor lay last night with his new lady, and is not risen yet." Lü Bu was in a fury. He slipped round behind Dong Zhuo's bedchamber to spy. Diaochan had risen and was combing her hair below the window when she saw in the pool outside it the reflection of a man, tall and broad, with a coronet binding up his hair. She stole a look, and it was Lü Bu. She knit her brows on purpose and put on a sorrowful face, and wiped her eyes again and again with a scented silk. Lü Bu watched a long while, then went out, and in a little while came in again. Dong Zhuo was by now sitting in the middle hall, and seeing him come he asked whether all was well outside. Lü Bu said it was, and stood in attendance at his side. Dong Zhuo was at his food, and Lü Bu, watching out of the corner of his eye, saw a woman behind the embroidered blind going to and fro and peeping out, showing half her face and sending him her looks. He knew it was Diaochan, and his senses swam. Dong Zhuo saw the look of him, and grew suspicious and jealous. "If there is nothing to report, Fengxian, you may go," he said, and Lü Bu went out sullenly.


From the day he took Diaochan, Dong Zhuo was so bewitched by her beauty that for a month and more he never came out to see to any business. He fell slightly ill, and Diaochan nursed him night and day without once undressing, and bent herself to please him in everything, so that he grew fonder of her than before. Lü Bu came in to ask after his health and found Dong Zhuo asleep. Diaochan leaned half out from behind the bed and looked at Lü Bu. She pointed to her heart and then to Dong Zhuo, and the tears would not stop. Lü Bu's heart broke within him. Then Dong Zhuo's eyes opened dimly, and he saw Lü Bu staring fixedly behind the bed. He turned and looked, and there was Diaochan standing behind it. A great rage took Dong Zhuo, and he railed at Lü Bu: "You dare to toy with my own concubine!" He called his men to put him out, and forbade him the hall from that day on.


Lü Bu went home angry and brooding, and meeting Li Ru on the road he told him the whole story. Li Ru hurried in to Dong Zhuo. "You mean to take the realm, Grand Preceptor. Why rebuke the Marquis of Wen for so small a fault? If his heart turns, the great design will be lost." Dong Zhuo said: "What is to be done?" Li Ru said: "Call him in tomorrow morning, give him gold and silk, and comfort him with kind words. The matter will settle itself." Dong Zhuo did as he was advised. Next day he had Lü Bu called into the hall and comforted him. "I was sick the other day and my wits wandered, so I spoke amiss and wounded you. Do not take it to heart." And he gave him ten jin of gold and twenty bolts of brocade. Lü Bu thanked him and went away, but though his body was at Dong Zhuo's side his heart was bound to Diaochan.


When Dong Zhuo was well again he went up to court to deliberate, and Lü Bu attended him with his halberd. Seeing Dong Zhuo deep in talk with the Emperor, he took his chance and carried the halberd out by the inner gate, got to horse and rode straight for the Chancellor's residence. He tied his horse before the gate, took the halberd into the back hall, and found Diaochan. She said: "Go and wait for me by the Fengyi Pavilion in the back garden." Lü Bu took the halberd and went, and stood below the pavilion beside the winding rail. After a long while Diaochan came, parting the flowers and brushing the willows aside, for all the world like an immortal out of the moon, and said to him weeping: "I am no true daughter of Minister Wang's, but he treated me as his own. When I first saw you, General, and was promised to keep your house, my whole life's wish was granted. Who would have thought the Grand Preceptor would conceive a wicked desire and defile me? I longed to die on the spot, but I had never said farewell to you, and so I bore the shame and lived on. Now that I have seen you my wish is fulfilled. This body is fouled and may serve a hero no longer. Let me die before your eyes, and show you my heart!" With that she laid hold of the winding rail and sprang toward the lotus pool, but Lü Bu caught her in his arms and wept over her. "I have long known your heart! I grieved only that we could never speak together!" Diaochan clung to him. "In this life I cannot be your wife. Let us look to meet in the life to come." Lü Bu said: "If I cannot have you to wife in this life, I am no hero!" She said: "Every day I live is like a year. Have pity and save this girl." Lü Bu said: "I have stolen a moment to come here, and I fear the old traitor may suspect me. I must be gone quickly." Diaochan caught at his coat. "If you are so afraid of the old traitor, I shall never see the light of day again!"


Lü Bu stood still. "Give me time to think out a good plan." And with that he took up his halberd to go. Diaochan said: "In my inner chamber your name came to my ears like thunder, General, and I took you for the man of the age. Who would have thought you served another!" And the tears fell from her like rain. Shame covered Lü Bu's face. He leaned the halberd aside again, turned and took her in his arms, and comforted her with soft words. The two clung close together and could not bear to part.


Now Dong Zhuo, in the hall of audience, turned his head and could not see Lü Bu. Suspicion rose in him. He took a hurried leave of the Emperor, got into his carriage and drove home, and there was Lü Bu's horse tied before the gate. He asked the gatekeeper, who answered: "The Marquis of Wen has gone into the back hall." Dong Zhuo sent his people away and went straight into the back hall, and found no one there. He called for Diaochan, and she was not to be seen either. He asked the waiting-women in haste, and they said: "Diaochan is in the back garden, looking at the flowers."


Dong Zhuo went into the back garden and found them there, Lü Bu and Diaochan talking together below the Fengyi Pavilion, with the halberd leaning at one side. In his anger he gave a great shout. Lü Bu saw him come and started back in alarm, then turned and ran. Dong Zhuo snatched up the halberd and came after him with it leveled. But Lü Bu ran fast, and Dong Zhuo was too fat to overtake him. So he hurled the halberd at him, and Lü Bu struck it down to the ground. Dong Zhuo picked it up and ran on, but by then Lü Bu was far away. As Dong Zhuo came out at the garden gate, a man came flying toward him and crashed against his chest, and down he went. Truly:
    His wrath rose up a thousand zhang on high;
    The fat bulk tumbled, in a heap to lie.
Who this man was, the next chapter will tell.

Now the man who had knocked Dong Zhuo down was Li Ru. He raised him up and brought him into the study, and there they sat. Dong Zhuo said: "What brought you here?" Li Ru said: "I had just reached the gate when I heard that you had gone into the back garden in anger, looking for Lü Bu. I hurried after you, and met him running out, crying: 'The Grand Preceptor means to kill me!' I ran into the garden to make peace, and blundered against you, my lord. I have earned death by it!" Dong Zhuo said: "The traitor is not to be borne! He toyed with my own concubine, and I swear I will have his life!" Li Ru said: "You are wrong there, my lord. At the feast of the broken cap-strings, King Zhuang of Chu would not call Jiang Xiong to account for toying with his own favorite, and afterwards, when the Qin troops had him hemmed in, it was Jiang Xiong who fought to the death to save him. Diaochan is no more than a woman, but Lü Bu is your own right hand and a bold captain. Take this chance and give her to him, and he will be so moved by the favor that he will repay you with his life. I beg you to think well on it." Dong Zhuo brooded a long while, then said: "There is reason in what you say. I will think of it."

Li Ru thanked him and withdrew. Dong Zhuo went into the back hall, called Diaochan and asked her: "What is this intrigue of yours with Lü Bu?" Diaochan said weeping: "I was in the back garden looking at the flowers when Lü Bu came on me suddenly. I was starting away from him in fright when he said: 'I am the Grand Preceptor's son. Why run from me?' Then he took up his halberd and drove me as far as the Fengyi Pavilion. I saw that his mind was impure, and feared he would force me, so I would have thrown myself into the lotus pool and made an end of it, but the wretch caught me in his arms. I hung between life and death until you came and saved me." Dong Zhuo said: "What if I were to give you to Lü Bu?" Diaochan cried out in dismay: "I have served a man of rank, and now I am to be handed down to a household slave. I will die before I bear that shame!" And she snatched the fine sword that hung on the wall to cut her own throat.

Dong Zhuo wrested the sword from her in alarm and took her in his arms. "I jest!" Diaochan sank against his breast, hid her face and wept: "This must be Li Ru's doing! He and Lü Bu are close friends, and so he has laid this plan, caring nothing for your honor nor for my life. I could eat his flesh alive!" Dong Zhuo said: "How could I bear to part with you?" Diaochan said: "Though you love me as you do, I dare not stay here long, for Lü Bu will surely work me harm." Dong Zhuo said: "Tomorrow you and I go back to Meiwu together, and we shall be merry there. Fear no more." Then Diaochan dried her tears and bowed her thanks. Next day Li Ru came in and said: "Today is a lucky day. Diaochan may be sent to Lü Bu." Dong Zhuo said: "Lü Bu and I stand as father and son, and it would not be fitting to give her to him. I will do no more than pass over his fault. Tell him my mind, and comfort him with good words." Li Ru said: "You must not let a woman befool you, Grand Preceptor." Dong Zhuo's face changed. "Would you give your own wife to Lü Bu? Not another word of Diaochan. Speak of it again and I will have your head!" Li Ru went out, and looking up to heaven he sighed: "We shall all of us die by a woman's hand!" Later men, reading thus far, made a poem in lament:
    A red skirt bore the Minister's design;
    No spear was drawn, no soldier stood in line.
    Three fights at Hulao spent their strength in vain;
    At Fengyi Pavilion rose the victor's strain.

That same day Dong Zhuo gave the order to return to Meiwu, and the whole body of officials bowed him away. Diaochan, from the carriage, saw Lü Bu far off in the press of people with his eyes on her, and half covered her face as though she were weeping bitterly. When the carriage had gone far, Lü Bu drew his rein on a knoll and watched the dust of it, sighing in grief and bitterness. Then he heard a man behind him ask: "Marquis of Wen, why do you not follow the Grand Preceptor, but stand here gazing after him and sighing?" Lü Bu turned, and it was Wang Yun, the Minister of the Masses.

When they had greeted each other Wang Yun said: "It is long since I saw you, General, for a slight sickness has kept my doors shut these days past. Today, with the Grand Preceptor's carriage going back to Meiwu, I had to come out, ill as I am, to see him away, and I count it a piece of luck to have met you. Tell me, what sets you sighing here?" Lü Bu said: "It is your daughter that I sigh for." Wang Yun feigned surprise. "All this while, and she is not yours yet, General?" Lü Bu said: "The old traitor has had her to himself this long while!" Wang Yun made a great show of astonishment. "I cannot believe such a thing!" Then Lü Bu told him the whole story. Wang Yun turned his face up and stamped his foot, and for a good space said nothing; at length he spoke: "I never thought the Grand Preceptor would do a thing so beastly!" And he took Lü Bu by the hand. "Come to my humble house, and we will talk of it." Lü Bu went home with him. Wang Yun led him into an inner room and set wine before him, and Lü Bu told over again, at length, what had passed at the Fengyi Pavilion. Wang Yun said: "The Grand Preceptor has defiled my daughter and robbed you of your wife, General, and the realm will laugh — not at him, but at you and me! I am old and worthless, and no more need be said of me. But that you, the hero of the age, should bear such shame!"

Lü Bu's anger blazed up, and he struck the table with a shout. Wang Yun said quickly: "My loose tongue is at fault, General. Let your anger cool." Lü Bu said: "I swear I will kill the old traitor and wipe out my shame!" Wang Yun clapped a hand over his mouth. "Say no more, General. I fear it will fall upon me as well." Lü Bu said: "Shall a man live stifled under another all his days?" Wang Yun said: "With such gifts, you are indeed no man for the Grand Preceptor Dong to hold within bounds." Lü Bu said: "I would kill the old traitor, but we stand as father and son, and I fear what later men would say of it." Wang Yun smiled a little. "Your own surname is Lü, General, and the Grand Preceptor's is Dong. Where was the love of father and son when he threw the halberd at you?" Lü Bu started up. "But for your words, Minister, I had near led myself astray!"

Wang Yun saw that his mind was made up, and pressed him: "If you, General, uphold the house of Han you are a loyal servant, and your name shall go down in the histories and the fragrance of it last a hundred ages; if you help Dong Zhuo you are a traitor, and the historian's brush will leave your stench to all the ages after." Lü Bu rose from his mat and bowed to the ground. "My mind is made up, Minister. Have no doubt of me." Wang Yun said: "I fear only that it may fail, and bring a great calamity on us instead." Then Lü Bu drew the blade at his belt, pricked his arm and swore upon the blood. Wang Yun knelt and thanked him. "If the altars of Han still stand, it will be of your giving, General. Let nothing leak out! When the hour comes I shall have a plan, and will send you word."

Lü Bu gave his word readily and went. Wang Yun then called in Shisun Rui, Supervisor of the Imperial Secretariat, and Huang Wan, Director of Retainers, to take counsel with him. Shisun Rui said: "His Majesty is but newly recovered of his sickness. Send a man with a ready tongue to Meiwu, that he may ask Dong Zhuo in for a council. At the same time put a secret edict of the Son of Heaven into Lü Bu's hands, and let him set armed men within the palace gate, so that Dong Zhuo is cut down as he is brought in. That is the best course." Huang Wan said: "Who will dare to go?" Shisun Rui said: "Li Su, the Cavalry Commandant, is of Lü Bu's own commandery, and carries a grudge that Dong Zhuo has never advanced him. Send that man, and Dong Zhuo will suspect nothing." Wang Yun approved, and he had Lü Bu called in to the council. Lü Bu said: "It was this same man who talked me into killing Ding Jianyang. If he will not go, I will have his head first." So they sent secretly for Li Su, and he came.

Lü Bu said: "In other days you talked me into killing Ding Jianyang and going over to Dong Zhuo. Now Dong Zhuo cheats the Son of Heaven above and grinds the people below, his guilt is brimming over, and the wrath of gods and men alike is on him. Carry the edict of the Son of Heaven to Meiwu and summon him to court. We will lay men in wait to cut him down, prop up the house of Han, and be loyal servants together. What do you say?" Li Su said: "I have long wished to be rid of the traitor, and only grieved that I had no man of like mind. Now that you are resolved, General, it is Heaven's gift. How should I dare prove false?" And he broke an arrow in pledge. Wang Yun said: "Carry this through, sir, and you need not doubt of high office."

Next day Li Su rode to Meiwu with a dozen horsemen. Word was brought that an edict had come from the Son of Heaven, and Dong Zhuo had him called in. Li Su entered and bowed. Dong Zhuo said: "What edict has the Son of Heaven sent?" Li Su said: "The Son of Heaven is newly healed of his sickness, and the edict calls the civil and military officers together in the Weiyang Hall, to deliberate upon yielding the throne to you, Grand Preceptor." Dong Zhuo said: "And what does Wang Yun say to it?" Li Su said: "Minister Wang has set men to build the Terrace of Abdication already, and waits only for you to come, my lord." Dong Zhuo was overjoyed. "Last night I dreamed that a dragon covered my body, and here is the good news. The hour must not be let slip!" So he ordered his four trusted generals, Li Jue, Guo Si, Zhang Ji and Fan Chou, to hold Meiwu with three thousand of the Feixiong troops, and that same day set his train in order to return to the capital. Turning to Li Su he said: "When I am Emperor, you shall be the Bearer of the Mace." Li Su bowed his thanks and called himself his subject.

Dong Zhuo went in to take leave of his mother, who was then past ninety. She asked: "Where are you going, my son?" Dong Zhuo said: "Your son goes to receive the throne of Han, and before long you will be the Empress Dowager!" His mother said: "These days my flesh has quivered and my heart has started in me. I fear it is no good omen." Dong Zhuo said: "You are to be the mother of the realm. Great news must have its forewarnings!" So he took his leave. As he was setting out he said to Diaochan: "When I am Son of Heaven, I will make you Noble Consort." Diaochan, who knew well enough how the matter stood, feigned delight and bowed her thanks.

Dong Zhuo came out of the stronghold and took his carriage, with his escort before and behind, and set out for Chang'an. He had not gone thirty li when a wheel of the carriage broke, so he got down and took to horse. Before another ten li the horse screamed and plunged and snapped its bridle. Dong Zhuo asked Li Su: "A wheel broken, a bridle parted. What omen is this?" Li Su said: "It is the omen that you are to receive the throne of Han, Grand Preceptor, putting off the old for the new, and that you will ride hereafter in a jade carriage and on a golden saddle." Dong Zhuo was pleased and believed him.

Next day, as they went, a wild wind sprang up of a sudden and a dark mist hid the sky. Dong Zhuo asked Li Su: "What sign is this?" Li Su said: "You are to mount the dragon throne, my lord, and there must be red light and purple mist to set off the majesty of Heaven." Again Dong Zhuo was pleased and doubted nothing. When he came to the city the whole body of officials came out to meet him. Only Li Ru lay sick at home and could not come. Dong Zhuo went on to the Chancellor's residence, and Lü Bu came in to congratulate him. Dong Zhuo said: "When I come to the throne you shall command all the armies of the realm." Lü Bu bowed his thanks and lay down for the night before Dong Zhuo's tent. That night a dozen children were singing out beyond the walls, and the wind carried the song into the tent:
    A thousand li of grass,
    How green it grows!
    Ten days are up above,
    No life it knows! *
The tune was mournful. Dong Zhuo asked Li Su: "What does it foretell, good or ill?" Li Su said: "It means only that the house of Liu falls and the house of Dong rises."

At dawn next day Dong Zhuo set his train in order and went up to court. On the way he saw a Daoist in a blue robe and a white headcloth, holding a long pole with a strip of cloth a zhang long bound to it, and at either end of the cloth was written the character for "mouth" *. Dong Zhuo asked Li Su: "What does the Daoist mean by that?" Li Su said: "Must be a crazy man." And he called the guard to drive him off. Dong Zhuo went on to the court, where the officers in their court robes met and saluted him along the road, while Li Su walked beside the carriage with a sword in his hand. At the Northern Side Gate the soldiers were all held outside, and only the twenty-odd men of the carriage went in with him. Dong Zhuo saw from afar Wang Yun and the others standing at the door of the hall, each with a sword in his hand, and asked Li Su in alarm: "What do the swords mean?"

Li Su made no answer, but pushed the carriage straight in. Wang Yun cried aloud: "The traitor is here! Where are the guards?" A hundred men and more came out from either side, with halberds and leveled lances, and thrust at him. Dong Zhuo wore mail beneath his robe and the points would not go through, but he took a wound in the arm and fell from the carriage, crying: "Where is my son Fengxian?" Lü Bu came out from behind the carriage and said: "By edict I strike the traitor down!" One thrust of the halberd went clean through the throat, and Li Su had the head off in his hand. Then Lü Bu held the halberd in his left hand, drew the edict from his breast with his right, and shouted: "By edict the traitor Dong Zhuo is put down. No other man shall be questioned!" And the officers all cheered. Later men made a poem in lament for Dong Zhuo:
    Had it gone through, the throne had been his seat;
    Had it gone wrong, a rich man's safe retreat.
    Who knew that Heaven keeps no partial way?
    Meiwu scarce finished, he in ruin lay.

Now Lü Bu raised his voice: "It was Li Ru who helped Dong Zhuo to all his cruelties! Who will take him?" Li Su answered that he would go. Then a shouting was heard outside the palace gate, and word came that Li Ru's own servants had bound him and brought him in. Wang Yun had him taken bound to the market place and beheaded, and Dong Zhuo's body set up in the open street for a warning. The body was so fat that the soldiers who watched it put a light in the navel for a lamp, and the grease ran over the ground. All who passed flung the head about with their hands and trod the body under their feet. Wang Yun further ordered Lü Bu, with Huangfu Song and Li Su, to take fifty thousand men to Meiwu, seize Dong Zhuo's goods and household, and set them down in a register.

Now Li Jue, Guo Si, Zhang Ji and Fan Chou, hearing that Dong Zhuo was dead and that Lü Bu was on his way, led off the Feixiong troops and rode through the night for Liang Province. Lü Bu came to Meiwu, and his first act was to take Diaochan. Huangfu Song gave orders that the children shut up in the stronghold should all be set free. But every kinsman of Dong Zhuo's, old and young alike, was put to death, including his mother. His brother Dong Min and his nephew Dong Huang were beheaded and their heads set up. They registered what was hoarded in the stronghold — some hundreds of thousands in gold, with silks and pearls, jewels and vessels and grain past all counting — and brought back their report to Wang Yun. He feasted the soldiers richly and laid out a banquet in the hall of state, where he gathered the officers together and they drank to the victory.

In the midst of the feasting word was brought: "Dong Zhuo's body lies exposed in the market, and a man has thrown himself upon it and weeps aloud." Wang Yun said angrily: "Dong Zhuo has met his punishment, and every gentleman and commoner rejoices in it. Who is this, that dares to weep over him?" And he called the guards: "Bring him before me!"

In a little while the man was brought in, and all the officers started at the sight of him, for it was Cai Yong, the Palace Attendant. Wang Yun rebuked him: "The traitor Dong Zhuo has met his punishment this day, and it is a great good fortune for the state. You are a servant of Han. Why do you not rejoice with the rest, but weep instead over a traitor?" Cai Yong owned his fault. "I have little ability, but I know what righteousness asks, and would never turn my back on the state for Dong Zhuo's sake. It was only that for a moment I remembered that he had known my worth, and the tears came before I was aware of it. I know the fault is great. Brand my face and cut off my feet if it must be, but let me finish the history of Han and atone for my guilt by it, and I shall count myself fortunate."

The officers, who valued Cai Yong's gifts, all pleaded hard for him, and Ma Midi, the Grand Tutor, said privately to Wang Yun: "Bojie's talent is unequaled in the age. Let him finish the history of Han, and it will be a glorious work. His filial conduct, besides, has long been famous, and if you kill him out of hand you will lose the goodwill of men." Wang Yun said: "Emperor Wu of old did not put Sima Qian to death, but set him to write his history, and a book of slanders has come down to later ages for it. The fortunes of the state are low now and the government in disorder, and we cannot have a flatterer holding the brush at the young sovereign's elbow, to leave us under his sneers." Ma Midi went out without a word, and said in private to the rest: "Wang Yun will leave no posterity! Good men are the mainstay of a state, and their writings are its law. Destroy the mainstay and throw away the law, and how shall the state stand long?"

Wang Yun would not hear Ma Midi, but had Cai Yong put in prison and strangled there, and the scholars and officers of the court all wept for him. Later men argued that Cai Yong did wrong to weep for Dong Zhuo, but also that Wang Yun went too far in killing him. There is a poem in lament:
    Dong Zhuo held all and gave his malice rein;
    Why did Cai Yong himself his death ordain?
    In those days Zhuge Liang at Longzhong lay;
    Would he have stooped a traitor to obey?

Now Li Jue, Guo Si, Zhang Ji and Fan Chou had fled to Shaanxi and settled there, and they sent a man to Chang'an with a memorial asking pardon. Wang Yun said: "In every evil of Dong Zhuo's these four had a hand. All others in the realm shall be pardoned, but not these four." The messenger carried word back to Li Jue, who said: "If no pardon is to be had, let each man flee for his life." Then Jia Xu, his counselor, said: "If you abandon your men and travel alone, a mere village chief could put you in bonds. Better draw the men of Shaanxi together, join them to your own companies, and cut your way into Chang'an to avenge Dong Zhuo. If we carry it through we shall hold the court and set the realm in order, and if we are beaten it will not be too late to run then."

Li Jue and the rest thought well of this, and set a rumor going through Xiliang that Wang Yun meant to sweep the region clean of its people. Fear ran through them all. Then the four declared: "Rather than die for nothing, will you follow us and rise?" And every man was willing. So they gathered more than a hundred thousand, divided them into four columns, and came driving upon Chang'an. On the road they met Niu Fu, Dong Zhuo's son-in-law and a General of the Household, with five thousand men, on his way to avenge his wife's father. Li Jue joined forces with him and set him in the van, and the four came on one behind another.

Wang Yun heard that the Xiliang troops were coming, and took counsel with Lü Bu. Lü Bu said: "Rest easy, Minister. Such rats are of no account!" So he took Li Su and led the army out against them. Li Su led the van and came face to face with Niu Fu, and there was a great slaughter. Niu Fu, unable to stand against him, drew off beaten. But that night, at the second watch, Niu Fu stormed his camp and caught him unready. Li Su's men broke and scattered, and he fled above thirty li and lost more than half his force before he came in to Lü Bu. Lü Bu shouted: "How dare you break the spirit of my army!" And he had Li Su beheaded and the head hung up at the gate of the camp.

Next day Lü Bu brought his men up against Niu Fu. And how should Niu Fu stand against him? He was heavily beaten again and fled. That night he called in Hu Chi'er, a man he trusted, and said: "Lü Bu is too bold and too strong, and there is no standing against him. Better keep it from Li Jue and the other three, take the gold and pearls, and leave the army with a few of our own people." Hu Chi'er agreed, and that same night they gathered the gold and pearls and went out of the camp with three or four in their train. They were about to cross a river when Hu Chi'er, coveting the treasure, killed Niu Fu and carried the head to Lü Bu. Lü Bu asked how it had come about, and the men of the party informed against him: "Hu Chi'er murdered Niu Fu to get his gold and jewels." Lü Bu was incensed, and had Hu Chi'er put to death on the spot. Then he led his men forward and came full upon Li Jue's companies, and before they formed he leveled his halberd, put his horse at them and waved his men on. Li Jue's troops could not withstand the charge, and fell back above fifty li and made their camp against a hill. There Li Jue called in Guo Si, Zhang Ji and Fan Chou, and said: "Lü Bu is brave but no strategist, and we need not fear him. I will hold the mouth of the valley with my men and draw him out to fight every day. You, General Guo, take your men round and strike at his rear the way Peng Yue harried Chu, sounding the gongs to advance and beating the drums to draw off. And you two, Zhang Ji and Fan Chou, divide your men into two columns and make straight for Chang'an. He cannot guard front and rear at once, and he will be soundly beaten." And they all took his plan.

Now Lü Bu drew up his men below the hill, and Li Jue led his out to offer battle. Lü Bu charged in a rage, and Li Jue fell back up the slope. Arrows and stones came down from the hill like rain, and Lü Bu's men could not advance. Then word came that Guo Si was cutting in at the rear, so Lü Bu turned hastily to meet him, but the drums rolled out and Guo Si's men had drawn off already. He was just calling his own men back when the gongs sounded and Li Jue's came on again, and before he could close with them Guo Si was at his back once more. Then, when Lü Bu turned upon Guo Si, the drums beat and he was gone, until Lü Bu's breast was bursting with rage. So it went for several days, and he could neither force them to a battle nor get any rest.

While Lü Bu was still fuming a rider came at speed with word that Zhang Ji and Fan Chou had fallen upon Chang'an with their two columns, and that the capital was in grave danger. He hurried back with his men, and Li Jue and Guo Si came cutting in behind him. With no mind to stand and fight he only pressed on, losing a good many men and horses. By the time he came under the walls of Chang'an the rebels lay thick about it as clouds and rain, with the city ringed round, and his troops fared badly in the fighting. His own soldiers feared his harsh and violent temper, and many of them went over to the rebels, which troubled Lü Bu greatly.

Some days later Li Meng and Wang Fang, remnants of Dong Zhuo's party, turned traitor within the city and opened a gate by stealth, and the four rebel columns poured in together. Lü Bu charged this way and that but could not hold them. With some hundreds of horse he rode to the Qingsuo Gate and called to Wang Yun: "Things are desperate! Mount, Minister, and come out through the pass with me, and we will look for a better way." Wang Yun said: "If the altars of the land lend us their power and the state can be set at peace, that is all I wish for. If it may not be, I will lay down my life. I will not run from danger to save myself. Carry my farewell to the lords east of the pass, and bid them strive with the state ever in their thoughts!"


Lü Bu urged him again and again, but Wang Yun would not go. Before long the flames at every gate were going up to the sky, and Lü Bu had no choice but to leave his household behind and gallop out through the pass with a hundred horse and more, to take refuge with Yuan Shu. Li Jue and Guo Si loosed their troops to plunder, and Chong Fu the Minister of Ceremonies, Lu Kui the Minister Coachman, Zhou Huan the Grand Herald, Cui Lie the Colonel of the City Gates and Wang Qi the Colonel of Picked Cavalry all died in the calamity that fell upon the state. The rebels pressed hard about the inner court, and the attendants begged the Son of Heaven to go up on the Xuanping Gate and quiet the tumult. When Li Jue and the rest saw the yellow canopy they held their men back and cried: "Long live the Emperor!" Emperor Xian leaned from the tower and asked: "You did not wait for leave, but entered Chang'an on your own authority. What do you mean to do?" Li Jue and Guo Si looked up and answered: "The Grand Preceptor Dong was a pillar of Your Majesty's state, and Wang Yun murdered him without cause. We have come only to avenge him, and would not dare rebel. Let us but see Wang Yun, and we will draw our men off."

Wang Yun was at the Emperor's side, and hearing this he said: "What I did, I did for the altars of the land. It has come to this now, and Your Majesty must not ruin the state to spare me. Let me go down to the two traitors." The Emperor wavered, unable to bear it. Then Wang Yun leaped from the tower of the Xuanping Gate and cried aloud: "Wang Yun is here!" Li Jue and Guo Si drew their swords and shouted at him: "What was the Grand Preceptor's crime, that he should be killed?" Wang Yun said: "The crimes of the traitor Dong Zhuo filled heaven and spanned the earth beyond all telling. On the day he was put to death every gentleman and commoner of Chang'an rejoiced together. Have you alone not heard?" Li Jue and Guo Si said: "Say the Grand Preceptor was guilty. What was our crime, that you would not pardon us?" Wang Yun cursed them: "Rebels! Why so many words? I, Wang Yun, am ready to die today!" The two traitors raised their hands and killed him there below the tower. A historian wrote a poem in his praise:
    When Wang Yun set his stratagem in play,
    The traitor Dong Zhuo's power passed away.
    He bore his country's grief within his breast,
    And on his brow the cares of court were pressed.
    A noble spirit scaled the heavens' height,
    And loyalty struck through the stars of night.
    His soul and spirit to this very hour
    Go circling still about the Phoenix Tower.

The rebels, having killed Wang Yun, sent men to put his whole kindred to death, old and young alike, and every gentleman and commoner wept for them. Then Li Jue and Guo Si reasoned together: "We have come this far. If we do not kill the Son of Heaven and strike for the great prize now, when shall we?" And sword in hand, with a great shout, they forced their way within. Truly:
    The chief offender paid, the scourge scarce past,
    His creatures range at large, and woe comes fast.
What came of Emperor Xian's life, the next chapter will tell.


Chapter 10
Ma Teng raises an army to aid the royal house
Cao Cao goes to war to avenge his father

Now the two traitors Li Jue and Guo Si meant to murder Emperor Xian, but Zhang Ji and Fan Chou spoke against it: "It will not do. If we kill him today, men may not submit to us. Better to go on serving him as our sovereign, lure the lords in through the passes, and clip his wings first. Then we can kill him, and the realm will be ours for the taking." Li Jue and Guo Si took their advice and put up their swords. The Emperor called down from the tower: "Wang Yun has been put to death. Why have you not drawn off your men?" Li Jue and Guo Si said: "We have done the royal house good service but have been given no rank for it, so we dare not withdraw." The Emperor said: "What ranks would you have?"


Li Jue, Guo Si, Zhang Ji and Fan Chou each wrote out the titles he wanted and sent them up, and the Emperor could only comply. Li Jue was made General of Chariots and Cavalry and Marquis of Chiyang, with the office of Director of Retainers and the staff and axe. Guo Si was made General of the Rear, with the staff and axe as well, and the two were to hold the government of the court together. Fan Chou became General of the Right and Marquis of Wannian, and Zhang Ji General of Agile Cavalry and Marquis of Pingyang, with his troops stationed at Hongnong. The rest, Li Meng and Wang Fang among them, were each made a Colonel. Then they thanked the Emperor for his grace and led their troops out of the city. They gave orders besides to search for Dong Zhuo's remains, and some scraps of skin and bone were found. These were fitted into a body carved of fragrant wood, great funeral rites were held, and it was given the robes, cap and double coffin of a prince and carried on a chosen lucky day to Meiwu for burial. But when the hour came a great thunderstorm broke, the water lay several chi deep on level ground, and a thunderbolt split the coffin and flung the body out. Li Jue waited for clear weather and buried it again, yet that night the same thing happened. Three times they tried a new grave and could not bury it, until the lightning had consumed the last scrap of his skin and bone. Great indeed was Heaven's anger against Dong Zhuo!


Now that Li Jue and Guo Si held supreme power, they oppressed the people cruelly and secretly set trusted men of their own about Emperor Xian to watch his movements, so that he moved as though among thorns at every step. The officers of the court rose and fell at the two traitors' pleasure. To win men's good opinion they summoned Zhu Jun to court, made him Minister Coachman, and gave him a share in the government. One day word came that Ma Teng, Grand Administrator of Xiliang, and Han Sui, Inspector of Bing Province, were bearing down on Chang'an with more than a hundred thousand men, declaring that they came to punish the traitors. The two generals had in fact sent men into Chang'an beforehand and leagued themselves with three who were to act for them within the city, Ma Yu the Palace Attendant, Chong Shao the Counselor Remonstrant and Liu Fan the Left General of the Household, to plot together against the traitors' party. The three had secretly memorialized the Emperor, who made Ma Teng General of the Western Campaign and Han Sui General of the Western Garrison, and each had received a secret edict to join forces and punish the traitors.


When Li Jue, Guo Si, Zhang Ji and Fan Chou heard that the two armies were at hand, they met to decide how to resist them. The counselor Jia Xu said: "The two armies have come a long way. The only course is to keep behind deep ditches and high walls and hold fast against them. Within a hundred days their grain will be spent and they will have to draw off. Then lead your men after them, and you will take both generals." Li Meng and Wang Fang stepped forward. "That is a poor plan. Lend us ten thousand picked men, and we will have the heads off Ma Teng and Han Sui at once and lay them at your feet." Jia Xu said: "If you fight now you are sure to be beaten." Li Meng and Wang Fang cried with one voice: "If we two are beaten, you may have our heads, but if we win, you must forfeit yours to us." Jia Xu said to Li Jue and Guo Si: "Two hundred li west of Chang'an lie the Zhouzhi hills, where the road is steep and dangerous. Let Generals Zhang Ji and Fan Chou camp there and hold it behind strong walls. Then Li Meng and Wang Fang may be left to take their own men and meet the enemy." Li Jue and Guo Si did as he said and gave Li Meng and Wang Fang fifteen thousand horse and foot. The two went off in high spirits and made their camp two hundred and eighty li from Chang'an. When the Xiliang troops came up, the pair led their men out to meet them. The Xiliang army drew up in line of battle across the road, and Ma Teng and Han Sui rode out side by side, pointed at Li Meng and Wang Fang and reviled them: "Traitors to the state! Who will go and take them?"


Before the words were out a young general came flying from the line, his face like jade in a cap and his eyes like shooting stars, with a tiger's body and an ape's arms, a panther's belly and a wolf's loins, a long spear in his hand and a fine horse under him. This was Ma Chao, Ma Teng's son, styled Mengqi, just seventeen years old and matchless in valor. Wang Fang, thinking little of so young an opponent, put his horse forward to meet him. They had not fought many bouts when Ma Chao ran him through and he fell from his horse. Ma Chao turned his horse and was riding back when Li Meng, seeing Wang Fang killed, came galloping alone behind him. Ma Chao pretended not to notice. Ma Teng shouted from under the standard: "There is a man coming up behind you!"


The shout had not died away when Ma Chao was seen with Li Meng a prisoner across his saddle. He had known well that Li Meng was after him and had loitered on purpose. When the horse drew close and the spear came thrusting at him he swayed aside, so that Li Meng lunged at empty air, and as the two horses came level Ma Chao stretched out his long arm and took him alive out of the saddle. The soldiers, with no one left to lead them, fled at the sight. Ma Teng and Han Sui pressed the pursuit and won a great victory. They drove on to the mouth of the pass, camped there, and had Li Meng beheaded and his head set up as a warning.


When Li Jue and Guo Si learned that Ma Chao had killed both Li Meng and Wang Fang, they believed at last in Jia Xu's foresight. They kept to his plan now and thought only of holding the pass, letting the enemy challenge as he would and never going out to meet him. Sure enough, in less than two months the Xiliang army was short of grain and fodder alike, and its leaders were debating a retreat. Just then a servant of Ma Yu's household in Chang'an informed against his master, and against Liu Fan and Chong Shao with him, for being in league with Ma Teng and Han Sui outside and meaning to act for them within the city. Li Jue and Guo Si were furious. They seized every soul of the three households, old and young, master and servant, and beheaded them in the market place, and the heads of the three men were brought straight to the front and set up before the gate.


Ma Teng and Han Sui, with their grain spent and their plot within the city betrayed, could do nothing but break camp and retreat. Li Jue and Guo Si sent Zhang Ji with an army after Ma Teng and Fan Chou with another after Han Sui, and the Xiliang army was routed. Ma Chao fought desperately in the rear and beat Zhang Ji back. Fan Chou went after Han Sui and had all but overtaken him near Chencang, when Han Sui reined in and said to him: "You and I are men of the same district. Why so merciless today?" Fan Chou reined in too and answered: "I am under orders and may not disobey!" Han Sui said: "I came here for the state's sake as well. Why must you press me so hard?" At that Fan Chou turned his horse, called in his men and went back to camp, and let Han Sui go. But he had not counted on Li Bie, Li Jue's nephew, who saw him let Han Sui escape and went back to tell his uncle. Li Jue, in a great rage, was for marching against Fan Chou at once. Jia Xu said: "Men's minds are not yet settled, and to be forever taking up arms would be most unwise. Better lay a feast and invite Zhang Ji and Fan Chou to celebrate the victory, then seize Fan Chou at the table and behead him. Nothing could be easier."


Li Jue was delighted. He laid the feast and invited Zhang Ji and Fan Chou, and the two generals came to it gladly. Halfway through the feast Li Jue suddenly turned grim. "Fan Chou, why have you been dealing with Han Sui and plotting rebellion?" Fan Chou was thunderstruck, and before he could answer men with swords and axes rushed out and struck off his head at the foot of the table. Zhang Ji fell on his face in terror. Li Jue raised him up and said: "Fan Chou plotted rebellion, and so I had him put to death. You are my own trusted friend. What have you to fear?" He put Fan Chou's troops under Zhang Ji's command, and Zhang Ji went back to Hongnong.


After Li Jue and Guo Si had beaten the Xiliang troops, not one of the lords dared call them to account. Jia Xu urged them again and again to give the people peace and to make friends of men of worth and standing, and from this time the court showed some faint signs of life. Then, unlooked for, the Yellow Turbans of Qing Province rose again, some hundreds of thousands of them in bands under many chiefs, plundering honest folk. Zhu Jun, the Minister Coachman, offered to recommend a man who could put the rebels down, and Li Jue and Guo Si asked who it was. Zhu Jun said: "If the rebels of Shandong are to be broken, it must be Cao Mengde and no other." Li Jue said: "Where is Mengde now?" Zhu Jun said: "He is Grand Administrator of Dong Commandery, and has a large force under him. Order him against the rebels, and they will be crushed in a matter of days." Li Jue was well pleased. He had an edict drafted that same night and sent a man with it to Dong Commandery, ordering Cao Cao to join Bao Xin, Chancellor of Jibei, against them. Cao Cao received the edict and joined forces with Bao Xin, and together they marched and struck the rebels at Shouyang. Bao Xin cut his way too deep into their ground and was killed. Cao Cao chased them as far as Jibei, where tens of thousands surrendered. He put the surrendered men in his van, and wherever his army came none failed to submit. In little more than a hundred days he had taken in over three hundred thousand soldiers who gave themselves up, with more than a million men and women besides. He picked out the best of them and named them the Qing Province troops, and the rest he sent back to their farms. From this time Cao Cao's name carried more weight by the day. When the report of the victory reached Chang'an, the court raised him to General of the Eastern Garrison.


In Yan Province Cao Cao set about drawing worthy and able men to him. An uncle and a nephew came to join him. The uncle was a man of Yingyin in Yingchuan, Xun Yu by name, Wenruo by style, and the son of Xun Kun. He had served Yuan Shao and now left him for Cao Cao, who talked with him, was greatly pleased, and said: "This is my Zifang!" And he made him a Major on his staff. The nephew, Xun You, styled Gongda, was a scholar known throughout the realm, who had once been a Gentleman of the Yellow Gate and afterwards gave up his office and went home. Now he came with his uncle, and Cao Cao made him instructor to the army. Xun Yu said: "I have heard of a worthy man in Yan Province, but I do not know where he is now." Cao Cao asked who it was, and Xun Yu said: "A man of Dong'e in Dong Commandery, Cheng Yu by name, styled Zhongde." Cao Cao said: "I too have long known him by name." He sent men to ask after him in his own district, and they found that he was studying in the hills. Cao Cao invited him with all courtesy, and when Cheng Yu came Cao Cao was overjoyed.


Cheng Yu said to Xun Yu: "I am an ignorant man who has seen little, and do not deserve your recommendation. But there is a man of your own district, Guo Jia by name, styled Fengxiao, who is one of the worthies of the age. Why not bring him in?" Xun Yu started. "I had almost forgotten him!" So he spoke to Cao Cao, who invited Guo Jia to Yan Province, and they discussed the affairs of the realm together. Guo Jia recommended a descendant of Emperor Guangwu in the direct line, a man of Chengde in Huainan, Liu Ye by name, styled Ziyang, and Cao Cao sent for him at once. Liu Ye in turn recommended two men: one of Changyi in Shanyang, Man Chong by name, styled Boning, and one of Wucheng, Lü Qian by name, styled Zike. Cao Cao had long known both by reputation, and took them as Assistant Officers in his army. Man Chong and Lü Qian together recommended a man of Pingqiu in Chenliu, Mao Jie by name, styled Xiaoxian, and Cao Cao made him an Assistant Officer too. A commander also came to join Cao Cao with several hundred men, a man of Juping in Taishan, Yu Jin by name, styled Wenze. Cao Cao saw that he was practiced with bow and horse and of uncommon skill at arms, and gave him a Major's command.


One day Xiahou Dun brought in a huge fellow to see Cao Cao. Asked who he was, Xiahou Dun said: "This is a man of Chenliu, Dian Wei by name, stronger and bolder than other men. He followed Zhang Miao once, but fell out with the men of his camp, killed some dozens of them with his own hand, and fled into the hills. I was out hunting when I saw him chase a deer across a mountain stream, and so I took him into my troop. I have brought him here to recommend him to you." Cao Cao said: "I can see from his great frame that he must be strong." Xiahou Dun said: "He once killed a man to avenge a friend and walked straight out through the crowded market with the head in his hand, and of some hundreds of people not one dared go near him. The pair of iron halberds he uses now weigh eighty jin, and he mounts with them under his arms and swings them as if they weighed nothing." Cao Cao bade Dian Wei show his skill there and then. Dian Wei took the halberds under his arms and galloped up and down. Just then the great banner before the tent was caught by the wind and rocked as if it must fall, and a crowd of soldiers could not hold it steady. Dian Wei got down from his horse, shouted them back, gripped the staff in one hand, and stood towering in the wind without stirring. Cao Cao said: "Here is Elai of old!" He made him Commandant of the Guard, and took off the brocade coat he was wearing and gave it to him, with a fine horse and a carved saddle. From this time Cao Cao had wise counselors on the civil side and fierce generals on the military, and his might overawed Shandong. He then sent Ying Shao, Grand Administrator of Taishan, to Langya Commandery to fetch his father Cao Song.


Cao Song had left Chenliu to escape the troubles and was living in retirement at Langya. On receiving the letter he set out at once for Yan Province with his younger brother Cao De and all his household, old and young, more than forty persons, with over a hundred attendants and as many carts. His road lay through Xu Province, whose Grand Administrator, Tao Qian, styled Gongzu, was a mild and kindly man, honest and sincere. He had long wished to make a friend of Cao Cao and had found no way to it. Learning now that Cao Cao's father was passing through, he went out to the border to meet him, bowed twice in respect, spread a great feast, and entertained him for two days. When Cao Song was ready to go on, Tao Qian saw him beyond the outer wall himself, and sent the Commandant Zhang Kai with the five hundred men of his command to escort him.


Cao Song and his household had come as far as the road between Hua and Fei when, at the turn of summer into autumn, a sudden heavy rain came on, and they had to seek a night's lodging in an old temple. The monks took them in. Cao Song settled his family and told Zhang Kai to quarter his men in the two side galleries. The soldiers' clothes and gear were soaked through by the rain, and they were all grumbling. Zhang Kai called his headmen aside to a quiet place and said: "We were Yellow Turbans once. We gave ourselves up to Tao Qian because we had to, and have got nothing by it. Now here is Cao Song with baggage and carts past counting. Riches are within our grasp. Tonight at the third watch let us all cut our way in, kill Cao Song and his whole household, take the goods, and go off together to the hills as outlaws. What do you say to it?"


They all agreed. That night, with the wind and rain still unabated, Cao Song was sitting up when a great shouting broke out on every side. Cao De took his sword and went out to look, and was run through. Cao Song hurried with one of his concubines round behind the abbot's quarters, meaning to climb the wall and escape, but she was too fat to get over it. In his panic he hid with her in the privy, and there the soldiers killed him in the tumult. Ying Shao barely escaped with his life and went off to Yuan Shao. Zhang Kai killed every one of Cao Song's household, took the goods, set fire to the temple, and fled with his five hundred men to Huainan. Later men made a poem on it:
    Cao Cao, the treacherous hero all men praise,
    Slew the whole house of Lü in former days.
    Now all his kin by other men are killed;
    So Heaven's law repays, its round fulfilled.


Some of Ying Shao's soldiers who had escaped with their lives brought the news to Cao Cao. When he heard it he fell to the ground weeping, and those about him raised him up. He ground his teeth and said: "Tao Qian loosed his soldiers to kill my father. He and I cannot both live under heaven! I will raise my whole army and lay Xu Province waste, and only then shall I be avenged!" He left Xun Yu and Cheng Yu with thirty thousand men to hold the three counties of Juancheng, Fan and Dong'e, and with all the rest came driving upon Xu Province. Xiahou Dun, Yu Jin and Dian Wei led the van. Cao Cao gave orders that wherever a city was taken, every one of its people should be slaughtered in vengeance for his father. Bian Rang, the Grand Administrator of Jiujiang, was a close friend of Tao Qian's, and on hearing that Xu Province was in danger he came with five thousand men of his own to its relief. Cao Cao flew into a rage at the news, and sent Xiahou Dun to cut him off on the road and kill him.


At this time Chen Gong was an Assistant Officer of Dong Commandery, and he too was a close friend of Tao Qian. Hearing that Cao Cao had raised his army for vengeance and meant to kill all the people, he rode through the night to see him. Cao Cao knew that he came to plead for Tao Qian and would have refused, but he could not forget what he owed him, and so had him asked into the tent. Chen Gong said: "I hear, Your Excellency, that you are bringing a great army down on Xu Province to avenge your father, and mean to kill all the people wherever you go. I have come to offer a word of counsel. Tao Qian is a good and upright man, not the kind to put profit before honor. Your father's death was Zhang Kai's wickedness and no fault of Tao Qian's. And what wrong have the people of the province and its counties done you? No good can come of killing them. I beg you to think twice before you act." Cao Cao said angrily: "You cast me off once and went your way. How have you the face to come before me again? Tao Qian has killed my whole family, and I have sworn to tear out his gall and cut out his heart to slake my hatred! You may plead his cause all you like, but what can you do if I will not listen?" Chen Gong took his leave and went out, sighing: "Nor have I the face to go back to Tao Qian!" And he rode off to join Zhang Miao, Grand Administrator of Chenliu.


Now wherever Cao Cao's great army came it slaughtered the people and dug up the graves. When Tao Qian, in Xu Province, heard that Cao Cao had raised his army for vengeance and was killing the people, he looked up to heaven and wept aloud: "I have offended against Heaven, and brought this great calamity on the people of Xu!" He gathered his officers in haste to take counsel. Cao Bao said: "Cao Cao's troops are upon us. Are we to fold our hands and wait for death? Let me help you break them, Your Lordship."


Tao Qian had no choice but to lead his troops out to meet the enemy. Seen from afar, Cao Cao's army lay like spread frost and drifted snow, and in its center stood two white banners written large with four characters, Vengeance and Redress. The two armies drew up in line of battle. Cao Cao rode out before his line dressed in the plain white of mourning, raised his whip and railed at him. Tao Qian rode out too beneath his standard, bowed from the saddle and said: "It was because I wished to win your friendship, Your Excellency, that I charged Zhang Kai with the escort. I never thought he was still a robber at heart, and this is what came of it. Truly it was none of my doing, and I beg you to look into it." Cao Cao cursed him: "Old wretch! You killed my father, and you still dare talk nonsense! Who will take the old villain alive?" Xiahou Dun rode out at the word. Tao Qian fled back into his line in alarm, and Xiahou Dun was coming after him when Cao Bao leveled his spear and put his horse forward to meet him. The two horses had just closed when a wild wind sprang up, sand flew and stones rolled, both armies fell into disorder, and each side drew off its men.


Tao Qian went into the city and took counsel with his officers: "Cao Cao's force is too great to be withstood. I will bind myself and go to his camp and let him cut me to pieces as he will, to save the people of Xu." Before he had done speaking a man came forward and said: "Your Honor, you have long held Xu Province, and the people are grateful for your kindness. Cao Cao's troops are many, but they cannot break our city at a stroke. Hold fast with the people and do not go out. I have no great ability, but I have a small plan to offer that will leave Cao Cao without so much as a grave to lie in!" They were all astonished, and asked what the plan might be. Truly:
    He sought a friend and found a foe instead;
    Who knew that life would spring where hope lay dead?
Who this man was, the next chapter will tell.
